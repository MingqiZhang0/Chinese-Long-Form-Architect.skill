# Dialogue, Character Voice, and Social Action

## Core principle

Dialogue is not transcribed thought. It is social action performed under constraints.

A line may inform the reader while its in-world function is to request, refuse, test, recruit, conceal, threaten, soothe, save face, shift responsibility, delay, or force commitment.

## 1. Dialogue as action

For important exchanges, know what each speaker is trying to do to the situation or relationship.

Possible actions include:

- obtain information;
- verify a claim;
- deny responsibility;
- establish rank;
- preserve dignity;
- provoke disclosure;
- recruit;
- refuse;
- bargain;
- accuse;
- warn;
- reassure;
- distract;
- create plausible deniability;
- make the other person say something first;
- create a record;
- avoid a record.

Do not write dialogue as alternating paragraphs of information. When spoken information is later retold through a community, institution, or media chain, use `social-transmission-reputation.md`; the second telling should not automatically preserve the first.

### Pressure, counter-move, and turn

For a consequential exchange, identify three layers privately:

1. **surface proposition** - what the line literally says;
2. **social move** - what the speaker is trying to make happen now;
3. **protected stake** - what the speaker does not want exposed, lost, or conceded.

A strong exchange usually changes because the other person counters the move. The next line should not merely restate the previous position in new words. Let each turn alter at least one of:

- available information;
- leverage;
- face/status;
- emotional risk;
- topic ownership;
- commitment;
- plausible deniability;
- willingness to continue.

When two characters repeatedly circle the same proposition, make the repetition itself socially meaningful: stalling, intimidation, refusal to acknowledge, disbelief, ritual, or breakdown.

## 2. Speech acts and implicature

Characters frequently mean more, less, or something different from the literal proposition.

Use implication through:

- shared context;
- strategic omission;
- understatement;
- over-formality;
- topic shift;
- selective precision;
- answer to a different question;
- apparently irrelevant detail that the other party understands;
- refusal to confirm an assumption.

Subtext should remain inferable from motive and context. Obscurity alone is not depth.

## 3. Face and indirectness

People manage dignity, status, belonging, autonomy, and public image while speaking.

Indirectness should vary with:

- power difference;
- intimacy;
- danger;
- audience;
- institutional setting;
- cultural norm;
- whether the request threatens the other person's autonomy or reputation.

A subordinate may speak indirectly to a superior, but a superior may also become indirect when maintaining deniability or avoiding public humiliation.

## 4. Turn-taking and repair

Natural conversation contains structure beyond clean alternating turns.

Use selectively:

- interruption;
- overlap;
- delayed answer;
- self-repair;
- correction;
- clarification request;
- repetition with changed wording;
- abandoned sentence;
- topic return;
- strategic silence.

Repair reveals what participants consider ambiguous, dangerous, insulting, unbelievable, or socially costly.

Do not add verbal clutter by quota. Written dialogue is staged conversation, not a raw transcript.


### Paragraph ownership in dialogue

Treat paragraph breaks as attribution and interactional-ownership signals. A new speaker usually receives a new semantic paragraph, but the same speaker may keep dialogue, gesture, pause, and continuation together when they form one social action. A long monologue may require several paragraphs when its rhetorical move changes. Give silence its own paragraph only when the silence itself changes the social floor.

Use `paragraph-architecture.md` for boundary strength, paragraph clusters, and dialogue-heavy/polyphonic paragraph grammar.

## 5. Character voice matrix

Define recurring voices through deeper dimensions than catchphrases.

Track where useful:

- typical sentence length and compression;
- abstract versus concrete explanation;
- preferred evidence: numbers, stories, precedent, intuition, status, scripture, procedure;
- directness;
- tolerance for uncertainty;
- humor source;
- emotional naming level;
- profanity or taboo threshold;
- metaphor source domain;
- professional vocabulary;
- common evasion strategy;
- how they ask for help;
- how they refuse;
- how they lie;
- how they apologize;
- how they show affection;
- what they never say directly.

Voice is a pattern of attention and social behavior expressed through language.

## 6. Audience design

The same character should not sound identical with everyone.

Let speech shift with:

- superior versus subordinate;
- stranger versus family;
- lover versus rival;
- public versus private setting;
- official record versus unrecorded conversation;
- safety versus surveillance;
- first meeting versus long familiarity.

Stable identity and situational adaptation should coexist.

## 7. Register, sociolect, and code-switching

Language can index:

- region;
- generation;
- profession;
- education;
- class;
- institutional membership;
- subculture;
- in-group status;
- migration or multilingual history.

Use these as social signals, not costume.

Code-switching can mark intimacy, exclusion, solidarity, authority, irony, emotional regression, or a shift in audience. Do not reduce multilingual speech to stereotype or phonetic caricature.

### Chinese particles, idioms, and fixed expressions

For Chinese dialogue, do not manufacture spoken naturalness by sprinkling `啊/吧/呢/嘛`, discourse markers, or four-character idioms uniformly across speakers. These forms carry stance, turn-management, register, conventional meaning, and speaker-specific habits. Use `chinese-grammar-idiom.md` for sentence-final particles, idiom syntax/register, and fixed-expression checks; this module still owns **why this particular speaker chooses them with this particular audience**.

A character may intentionally misuse, deform, quote, mock, or half-remember an idiom when that behavior is part of characterization. Make the deviation legible as character behavior rather than accidental author error.

## 8. Subtext and silence

Subtext is the gap between literal words and social action.

Silence can mean many things:

- refusal;
- shock;
- calculation;
- surrender;
- contempt;
- fear;
- permission for the other person to continue;
- inability to answer;
- nothing important at all.

Do not label every silence ominous. Let context carry meaning.

## 9. Exposition control

Characters should not explain shared knowledge merely because the reader needs it.

Prefer:

- disagreement over interpretation;
- verification of a disputed fact;
- instruction to someone who genuinely lacks knowledge;
- procedural necessity;
- correction;
- consequence that makes the rule visible;
- partial reference that the reader can infer from context.

If both characters know the information and have no reason to restate it, move exposition elsewhere.


## 10. Attribution, beats, and information economy

Attribution should keep the social floor legible without becoming a second performance layered on every line.

For Chinese fiction, do not import English dialogue-tag rules mechanically. Instead ask whether the reader can track the speaker and whether the chosen reporting verb contributes real information. Plain speech verbs are often enough. Elaborate verbs, adverbs, and action beats should earn their presence.

Use an action beat when it does independent work such as:

- changes distance or object state;
- reveals avoidance, impatience, control, fatigue, or divided attention;
- redistributes turn-taking pressure;
- establishes who is watching whom;
- creates a pause with causal or social meaning.

Avoid beat inflation: a gesture after every line produces stage-direction chatter.

Apply a **one-channel sufficiency test**. If dialogue already makes an emotion or intention legible, do not automatically repeat the same information in a beat, tag adverb, internal thought, and narrator explanation. A second channel should add, contradict, complicate, or reframe the first.

Bad pattern:

- hostile line;
- angry action beat;
- narrator states that the speaker is angry.

Better pattern:

- let one channel establish anger;
- use another only if it adds a different fact, such as fear, performance, or restraint.

Do not force subtext into every line. Direct speech is natural when the relationship, urgency, culture, or task makes directness cheap. Subtext matters because characters have incentives, not because obliqueness is automatically literary.

## 11. Dialogue audit

Ask:

- What is each speaker doing socially with each line?
- What do they want the other person to believe or do?
- What can they not safely say directly?
- Who controls topic changes?
- Who answers questions and who avoids them?
- Does power appear in turn-taking, formality, interruption, or record-making?
- Would this speaker phrase the same content differently to another person?
- Can the line be cut without changing action, relationship, rhythm, or voice?

### Blind-speaker test

Remove dialogue tags and names from several short excerpts. Identity need not always be instantly obvious, but recurring major characters should differ through reasoning, register, indirectness, rhythm, and social strategy rather than gimmicks.

### Polished-riddle test

If every intelligent character produces elegant, compressed, quotable lines under pressure, degrade the symmetry. Intelligence does not imply identical rhetoric.
