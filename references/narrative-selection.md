# Narrative Selection: What Deserves Page Time

Use this reference after the world can run causally but before planning chapters. Its purpose is to prevent a well-simulated story from becoming an exhaustive log.

## Core principle

The world may contain more than the prose shows, but **page-time legitimacy comes from the current scoped Project Poetics**, not from one universal kind of change.

Do not confuse:

- what exists or happens in project/world state;
- what the reader needs to experience directly;
- what another kind of novel would conventionally foreground.

Before selecting page time, load `project-poetics.md` and identify active **salience sources**. These may include event/decision, knowledge/inference, relationship, ordinary continuity, perception, memory, atmosphere/place, ritual/recurrence, social texture, formal movement, waiting/stasis, or other project-authorized sources.

A believable world can contain far more events than prose should show. A deliberately narrow or highly subjective work may also choose not to simulate broad offscreen autonomy beyond its causal needs.

## 1. Page-time / scene-worthiness test

Ask first: **What contribution is this unit supposed to make under the current Project Poetics?**

A moment may deserve full or substantial scene treatment because direct experience accomplishes one or more of the following:

- changes objective, option set, knowledge, relation, body, resource, status, or interpretation;
- lets a relationship or social arrangement be inhabited rather than merely reported;
- establishes or deepens a routine, season, place, object-use history, or familiar baseline whose value is present-tense rather than only future setup;
- reorganizes perception, attention, atmosphere, bodily experience, or memory;
- performs meaningful waiting, repetition, ritual, failed change, or deliberate stasis;
- makes language/form/narration itself part of the reading event;
- advances an inquiry, clue structure, hypothesis, or epistemic asymmetry when that capability is active;
- creates another project-authorized form of progression or experiential completion.

If none of the project's legitimate salience sources are active, summarize, imply, combine, or omit.

For an obsessive, subjective, social or interpretive project, selection may follow object relation, self-observation, witness-sensitive strategy or an explanatory framework gaining/costing plausibility. Use `project-poetics.md` to compile the local criterion. The deletion test asks whether removing the scene loses authorized experience, familiarity, memory organization or social context as well as plot information; a huge event can still deserve only summary.

Do **not** demand a durable/local delta simply to justify a scene. Do not turn ordinary texture into setup/payoff merely to make it look functional.

## 2. Backstage simulation

Important offscreen actors continue to act.

Track backstage when relevant:

- rivals investigating;
- institutions processing records;
- markets adjusting;
- wounds healing;
- rumors spreading;
- relationships cooling or deepening;
- training continuing;
- families and organizations pursuing local goals;
- adversaries adapting after observed tactics.

Do not automatically cut away to show these processes.

Show them when the reader needs:

- a new causal fact;
- a change in option set;
- an emotionally important perspective;
- evidence that cannot be learned more naturally elsewhere;
- a counterweight that prevents protagonist-centered world freeze.

Otherwise let their effects arrive later through consequences, reports, altered behavior, missing resources, or changed expectations.

## 3. Enter late, leave early - but only when safe

A scene does not need to show every approach, greeting, seating, departure, and return journey.

Enter after routine setup when:

- spatial orientation is already clear;
- the omitted action contains no important choice;
- relationship dynamics are already established;
- the omitted material would only announce what the scene is about.

Leave when the meaningful state change is complete. Do not force a final summary, ominous tag, or explanatory moral.

Do not cut across:

- motive required for fairness;
- a trust transition;
- an irreversible decision;
- spatial information required for action clarity;
- a causal step whose omission would make the outcome feel magical.

## 4. Select POV for information pressure, not reaction harvesting

Use a viewpoint because that character's perception, ignorance, stakes, local task, or choices make the event richer.

Do not switch POV merely to collect:

- another person's shock;
- praise for the protagonist;
- villain anger;
- redundant explanation;
- confirmation that a reveal was impressive.

A POV change is justified when it changes what can be known, misunderstood, risked, chosen, or materially experienced.

For macro-to-human hard cuts, the destination character may be brief or one-off, but give them a local reality independent of the spectacle: a task, body, relationship, inconvenience, job, route, fear, or obligation already in progress. The cut should reveal how a large event exists at human scale, not merely provide a face to gasp at it.

Do not alternate scales on a visible schedule. A sudden cut can feel narratively random while remaining causally honest.

Ask:

- Who has the most consequential uncertainty here?
- Whose choices determine the scene?
- Whose ignorance creates productive tension?
- Which perspective reveals a different social or institutional layer?
- If this is a one-shot viewpoint, what was this person doing before the plot entered their life?

## 5. Separate world importance from narrative importance

A world event can be objectively large but narratively brief.

A tiny event can deserve pages because it changes a relationship or interpretation.

Examples:

- a month of training may compress to paragraphs;
- one failed circulation cycle may deserve pages;
- a major army movement may arrive as a report if no viewpoint directly participates;
- a small bureaucratic motion may deserve a full scene if it changes legal control of a mountain, company, household, or title.

Allocate prose by the experience and salience sources the project has authorized, not spectacle, clock time, or change magnitude alone. Use `narrative-time-duration.md` when deciding whether an interval deserves scene, summary, ellipsis, pause, or dilation.

## 6. Let chapters select around an intelligible contribution

A chapter does not need one topic or one kind of movement, but its sustained attention should be intelligible under the current Project Poetics. “Movement” may be eventful, epistemic, relational, perceptual, temporal, atmospheric, formal, recurrent, or deliberately static.

Useful movements include:

- refusal becomes commitment;
- a baseline becomes suspicious;
- a plan gains a target;
- a private fact becomes public pressure;
- a relationship moves from transaction toward trust;
- a conflict changes from physical to procedural;
- a mystery changes from “what happened?” to “who knew?”;
- an apparently safe victory creates new attention.

Do not force every chapter to end on a cliffhanger. The larger unresolved pressure can supply propulsion.

## 7. Select for reader-question movement

`reader-model.md` owns the foreground-question stack and reader hypotheses. Use that state here only as a selection criterion.

A scene or chapter usually earns page time when it moves, complicates, transforms, retires, or productively delays an active reader question while also preserving causal truth. If the same foreground question persists through repeated units without new pressure, evidence, cost, or interpretation, compress or select a different window.

Do not invent a twist merely to change the question.

## 8. Preserve ordinary life without converting it into setup

Ordinary scenes may deserve page time because they:

- make a place inhabitable;
- establish habits that later gain emotional meaning;
- show class, work, family, money, maintenance, schedule, or etiquette;
- allow relationship changes too small for plot summary;
- create contrast before disruption;
- let consequences settle.

Do not retroactively require every ordinary detail to become a clue.

A bowl may remain a bowl. A rainy afternoon may remain a rainy afternoon.

## 9. Avoid feature-checklist scenes

Do not select a scene because the outline “needs”:

- one politics scene;
- one warm meal;
- one foreshadowing object;
- one training beat;
- one humor beat;
- one literary image.

Ask instead:

> What are these people compelled or inclined to do now, and which of those actions changes the story enough to deserve observation?

Desired qualities should emerge from causal life.

## 10. Do not show every intelligent move

Strategic credibility does not require showing all planning meetings.

Sometimes it is stronger to reveal competence through:

- an opponent already having prepared a countermeasure;
- a document arriving earlier than expected;
- resources already being moved;
- a character discovering the other side asked the right question offscreen;
- a later action that implies prior preparation;
- a normal-looking operation whose second-order diagnostic purpose becomes legible only after a later reveal.

It is legitimate to withhold a second-order objective when the visible action remains causally honest from the current viewpoint. Do not withhold the first-order motive that makes the action intelligible.

Show planning when the decisions themselves are dramatic, uncertain, relationally important, or necessary for reader fairness.

## 11. Arc-level selection

Before an arc, identify:

- the world processes that will continue regardless of page time;
- the few public events everyone will react to;
- the private events that change relationships;
- the hidden events whose effects can arrive later;
- the institutional decisions that alter options;
- which threads must remain backstage to prevent overcoverage.

An arc should feel larger than the scenes shown because unseen life continues between them.

## 12. Narrative-selection audit

Before drafting, ask:

- Why does this event deserve a scene rather than summary or ellipsis?
- What will be different after the reader experiences it directly?
- Could the same causal fact arrive more powerfully through later consequence?
- Am I showing this POV only to harvest reaction?
- Am I narrating travel, setup, or administration merely because it happened?
- Is an ordinary scene genuinely inhabited, or just carrying the plot between points?
- Have I accidentally made every offscreen process visible?
- Does the chapter change the reader's governing question?
- Which important processes remain alive backstage after the chapter ends?
