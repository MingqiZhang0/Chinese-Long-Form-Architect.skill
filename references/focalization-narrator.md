# Focalization, Narrator, and Information Camera

Use this reference when a story contains restricted POV, braided POVs, unreliable narration, hidden experiments, secrets that must remain fair, or scenes where the wrong camera position would destroy suspense.

## Core principle

Keep at least four things distinct: world/project truth, narrator statement, focal character perception/interpretation, and reader inference. First-person or close access does not make a character's self-explanation canonical psychological truth.

Separate **who speaks** from **who perceives**.

A narrator can speak in third person while the information remains tightly restricted to one character. A first-person narrator can recount an event with later knowledge. An omniscient narrator can still choose temporary restriction.

Treat focalization as an information-control system, not merely a pronoun choice.

## 1. Voice versus focalization

For every important passage, know privately:

- who is narrating or supplying the discourse;
- whose perception organizes the scene;
- what that focalizer can sense;
- what that focalizer knows or believes;
- what they are likely to notice first;
- what they would omit as normal;
- what they would avoid noticing or naming;
- whether the narration may state facts beyond that person's access.

Do not use third person as permission for accidental omniscience.

## 2. Camera permission

Choose the information regime deliberately.

### Internal focalization

Narration may enter the selected character's perceptions, thoughts, bodily sensations, memory, inference, and error, but must not reveal another mind or inaccessible fact as certainty.

Use fixed internal focalization when sustained uncertainty matters. Use variable internal focalization when changing perspective produces enough causal, emotional, or thematic gain to justify the switch.

### External focalization

Present observable behavior, speech, objects, and environmental evidence without direct access to private thought. This can intensify uncertainty, social reading, or estrangement.

### Zero or broadly omniscient focalization

The narrator may exceed any one character's knowledge, but should still regulate information for purpose. Omniscience is not an obligation to disclose everything.

Do not switch regimes invisibly merely to rescue exposition.

## 3. Perceptual filtering

A viewpoint is a selection function.

Filter description through:

- expertise;
- profession;
- class and education;
- bodily condition;
- fear, desire, shame, grief, attraction;
- familiarity with the place;
- current objective;
- cultural assumptions;
- personal taboo;
- recent experience.

Two people can witness the same room and construct different usable worlds from it.

Viewpoint difference should appear in salience, interpretation, and omission before it appears in decorative diction.

## 4. Information leakage

Before revealing a secret, ask:

- Is this information available to the current focalizer?
- If not, does the chosen narrator have established permission to provide it?
- Does the reveal destroy a live uncertainty that the story still needs?
- Is the narration withholding something the focalizer would obviously be thinking about?

The last case is also cheating. Restricted POV may hide unknown information, but should not create suspense by forcing a character to avoid their own decisive thought for no psychological reason.

### Hidden-process rule

If an antagonist is covertly testing prophecy, surveillance, a power, or a leak, do not enter a viewpoint that explicitly names the experiment before the intended reveal unless that disclosure is itself the point of the scene.

Let the first read remain compatible with an ordinary tactical objective.

## 5. Unreliable narration

Unreliability includes more than factual lying. A narrator/character may sincerely misread motive, identity, memory, relation, or the meaning of their own behavior. Preserve the difference between `what this voice says about itself` and `what the project authoritatively knows`, when the latter is even decided.

Do not treat unreliability as random falsehood.

Locate the source:

- limited perception;
- mistaken inference;
- memory distortion;
- self-protection;
- shame or denial;
- ideology;
- strategic deception;
- retrospective reconstruction;
- altered state;
- social performance.

The reader should later be able to distinguish, at least partly, between:

- what happened;
- what the narrator perceived;
- what the narrator concluded;
- what the narrator chose to say.

A narrator can be sincere and unreliable. A hesitant narrator can be more reliable than a confident one.

For retrospective confession, keep five layers separate: what happened, what the experiencing self sensed then, what that self believed then, what the narrating self says now, and what the reader can infer. Later vocabulary or shame may reorganize the account without changing an event. Reporting, interpreting and evaluating need not be equally reliable [research:lhn-unreliability-2013]. Do not certify either a self-report or its editorial opposite as psychological truth.

## 6. Free indirect discourse

Free indirect discourse can blend narratorial grammar with a character's judgments, deixis, emotional coloring, and vocabulary without repeated tags such as "she thought."

Use it to deepen interiority without stopping the scene, but preserve attribution clarity through local context and viewpoint consistency.

Do not let every viewpoint inherit the same authorial metaphor system.


## 7. Psychic distance

Focalization answers **whose access controls the passage**. Psychic distance answers **how close the narration currently sits to that consciousness**. Keep the two concepts separate.

Use a practical distance ladder rather than a rigid numeric score:

- **panoramic / authorial distance** - broad time, place, social field, or historical framing;
- **external-personal** - follows one character but mostly reports observable action;
- **close interpretive** - the character's judgments and priorities shape sentence selection;
- **free-indirect closeness** - narratorial and character diction blend;
- **immediate sensory-thought** - raw perception, bodily reaction, fragments, or thought-pressure with minimal narratorial mediation.

Movement along this ladder should serve attention and pressure. It does not need to be gradual by formula, but abrupt jumps need a reason the reader can feel.

Useful movements:

- pull back to establish scale, then move close when choice becomes personal;
- move outward after shock when the character dissociates or the story needs consequence at a wider scale;
- tighten distance when perception itself is the action;
- widen when the character's self-interpretation would hide a larger social fact the chosen narrator is allowed to show.

Avoid **distance whiplash**: panoramic commentary, raw interior fragment, and detached camera description alternating sentence by sentence with no governing rhythm.

### Chinese close-third and free-indirect prose

Chinese does not need English tense/person marking to signal every shift into a character's interior language. Closeness can emerge through:

- deixis and temporal orientation local to the character;
- vocabulary the character would actually use;
- evaluative particles and sentence shape;
- omitted thought tags when attribution is already stable;
- what is noticed, skipped, misnamed, or taken for granted.

Do not manufacture intimacy by turning every thought into a fragment or rhetorical question. Deep POV is a selection and syntax problem, not a punctuation costume.

## 8. POV switching

Switch POV because another perspective changes at least one of:

- causal understanding;
- emotional stakes;
- strategic information;
- social interpretation;
- thematic position;
- scale;
- reader expectation.

Do not switch merely because the current viewpoint cannot conveniently witness a needed fact. First ask whether that fact can remain offscreen, arrive through evidence, rumor, consequence, or later reconstruction.

After a switch, reorient the reader through enough of:

- person;
- place;
- time;
- immediate goal;
- active pressure.

Avoid mechanical round-robin POV rotation.


### Focal handoff and paragraph boundaries

A POV or focal shift creates boundary pressure but does not mechanically require a new paragraph. A strong shared external anchor may carry attention from one mind to another, and some modernist passages intentionally preserve phenomenological continuity across objective time changes. Any such boundary weakening must remain camera-honest and supply compensatory cohesion. Use `paragraph-architecture.md` for anchor-mediated handoff and deliberate misalignment.

## 9. Multiple views of one event

Retell the same event only when the second view changes the model rather than repeating content.

Useful differences include:

- one witness saw a cause another missed;
- one person misread an intention;
- the same action carries different social meaning;
- a hidden cost becomes visible;
- chronology changes interpretation;
- a previously trusted account becomes questionable.

A repeated event should earn its page time through reinterpretation.

## 10. Focalization audit

Ask:

- Who speaks here?
- Who perceives here?
- What can this person actually know?
- What would this person naturally notice?
- What would they misread?
- What are they motivated not to name?
- Did the narrator leak hidden-state information?
- Did the narrator hide an obvious thought only to preserve suspense?
- Would a POV switch deepen the scene, or merely make exposition easier?
- Does the scene still work if all inaccessible knowledge is removed?
- Is psychic distance changing because the scene needs it, or because the prose is searching for intensity?
- Does closeness come from character-specific perception and syntax rather than generic fragments?

### Camera-cheat test

If the scene's suspense collapses when every sentence is limited to established camera permission, the problem is structural, not stylistic.
