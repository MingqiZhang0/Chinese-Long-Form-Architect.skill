# Thematic Architecture and Polyphony

> SELECTABLE POETICS CAPABILITY. Use only when Project Poetics calls for a theme/question to be tested through genuinely competing lives, positions, institutions, or consequences. This module is not the universal owner of theme, and a project need not turn every theme into a debate matrix.

Use this reference when the novel should develop philosophical, moral, political, or existential depth through story rather than authorial speeches.

## Core principle

Treat theme as a durable question tested by lives, systems, and consequences rather than a message announced by the narrator.

The author may design the arena without forcing every voice to become a subordinate proof of one approved worldview.

Approved humanities findings may seed future thematic development, but a recurring sidecar interpretation is not automatically the novel's official theme. `story-observatory.md` may track idea genealogy; this file still owns how ideas become embodied narrative structure.

## 1. Theme question

Phrase important themes as questions with genuine pressure.

Weak:

> Freedom is important.

Stronger:

> If knowing the future creates responsibility for preventing it, how much freedom remains in choosing not to act?

A good thematic question generates different reasonable answers under different costs.

## 2. Position matrix

For a major theme, map several positions:

- belief;
- lived reason for holding it;
- what the belief helps the person survive or protect;
- what evidence supports it;
- what it fails to explain;
- what cost appears when acted upon;
- what event could make the holder revise it.

Do not assign one position only to villains and another only to admirable characters.

## 3. Embodied arguments

Let positions appear through:

- choices under pressure;
- institutions and laws;
- family expectations;
- sacrifice;
- professional routines;
- resource allocation;
- who bears externalized costs;
- what people refuse to do even when useful;
- what becomes normalized over time.

Dialogue may articulate ideas, but action should expose what the ideas mean in practice.

## 4. Polyphonic discipline

For central disagreements:

- give important positions their strongest non-caricatured form;
- let characters notice weaknesses in positions the author may personally favor;
- allow a character to be morally wrong about one issue and perceptive about another;
- avoid narrator commentary that closes the argument immediately after a scene;
- let different social locations generate different truths about the same institution.

Polyphony does not require moral equivalence. Evidence and consequences can be unequal without turning every dissenting voice into stupidity.

## 5. Counterexample rule

Whenever a theme seems to have become too easy, introduce or notice an honest counterexample already available in the world.

If the story appears to prove:

> prediction destroys freedom,

ask whether someone uses prediction to create new freedom for others.

If it appears to prove:

> sacrifice is noble,

ask who is repeatedly asked to sacrifice and who receives the benefit.

Counterexamples deepen the question; they do not need to reverse the whole thematic direction.

## 6. Thematic character change

Characters can change their answer without changing the theme.

Track:

- initial answer;
- experience that strains it;
- behavior that contradicts it;
- reinterpretation;
- revised answer;
- unresolved contradiction that remains.

A mature arc often ends with a more precise question rather than total certainty.

## 7. Institutions as arguments

Institutions embody assumptions about people.

Ask of laws, schools, religions, militaries, markets, clans, or guilds:

- What do they assume people will do if unconstrained?
- What behavior do they reward?
- What costs do they hide?
- What kind of person succeeds inside them?
- What problem did the institution originally solve?
- Does that problem still exist?

This lets philosophy emerge from structure.

## 8. Recurrence without sermon

Revisit the thematic question in different domains and scales:

- private relationship;
- workplace;
- institution;
- public crisis;
- supernatural rule;
- historical precedent.

Do not repeat the same dialogue. Let each recurrence expose a different trade-off.


## 9. Approved analytical findings

A post-chapter report from `chapter-humanities-analysis.md` may propose a philosophical or adjacent thematic line. Treat it as inert unless the user explicitly marks the finding `APPROVED_FOR_DEVELOPMENT`.

When approved:

- translate the abstract idea back into characters, institutions, choices, costs, counterexamples, and recurring situations;
- preserve competing positions;
- prefer prospective development over retroactive explanation unless the user asks to revise earlier prose;
- do not make the narrator or a favored character announce the report's interpretation as truth;
- mark the report item `INTEGRATED` only after actual narrative material carries the idea.

The report is a discovery tool, not an authority over the novel.

## 10. Audit

- Could at least two intelligent characters disagree for reasons rooted in lived experience?
- Does the story contain costs that complicate the protagonist's preferred answer?
- Are institutions carrying thematic assumptions, not just speeches?
- Does the narrator explain the moral immediately after the scene already showed it?
- Has one viewpoint been protected from serious counterexamples?
- Do thematic developments alter decisions, or exist only in dialogue?
