# Complexity Pruning and Narrative Attention

Use this reference when a project has many characters, factions, POVs, mysteries, objects, rules, or subplots and risks becoming dense without becoming deep.

## Contents

- Character-space allocation

## Core principle

Prune according to the current Project Poetics, not according to a universal preference for one protagonist, one plot, or maximum causal convergence. Multiplicity, digression, social breadth, repeated routines, or formal complexity may be constitutive rather than bloat.

Complexity is valuable when it creates new structure, not merely more items.

A world may contain thousands of people and processes. The narrative should spend attention only where distinct causal, emotional, social, thematic, or experiential value justifies the memory cost.

## 1. World complexity versus presented complexity

Do not simplify the world merely because the prose must remain legible.

Instead separate:

- **simulated complexity** - what exists and continues backstage;
- **presented complexity** - what receives names, scenes, explanations, POV, or active reader questions.

The first can be broad. The second must be curated.

## 2. Attention as scarce resource

Every new named element creates a retrieval burden.

The cost rises when an element has:

- similar name or role to another;
- rare appearances;
- long gaps;
- weak emotional distinction;
- no unique causal function;
- simultaneous introduction with many peers.

Do not ask whether an element is realistic enough to exist. Ask whether it deserves foreground representation.

## 3. Structural contribution

A foregrounded element should usually contribute distinctly to at least one of:

- causality;
- character change;
- relationship structure;
- strategic pressure;
- theme or moral contrast;
- world autonomy when active;
- social texture;
- scale;
- clue or payoff architecture;
- emotional memory;
- comic or tonal contrast.

One strong contribution can be enough. Do not demand every detail serve the main plot.

## 4. Node versus edge depth

Prefer deeper relationships among existing elements over endless new nodes.

Before adding another faction, mentor, assassin, prophecy, rank, artifact, or hidden mastermind, ask whether the desired complexity can come from:

- a changed alliance;
- conflicting incentives inside an existing organization;
- a historical relationship;
- information asymmetry;
- resource dependence;
- jurisdictional overlap;
- a mistaken model;
- one person belonging to two social worlds.

Ten disconnected factions are often shallower than three factions with history, trade, marriage, rivalry, and internal dissent.

## 5. Merge, demote, compress, remove

When an element underperforms, do not default to deletion.

Possible operations:

### Merge
Combine two roles when their separation creates no meaningful conflict, contrast, or independent life.

### Demote
Keep the person or process in the world but remove a proper name, POV, or dedicated subplot.

### Compress
Preserve consequences while summarizing the bridge.

### Remove
Delete elements whose existence adds no durable value and creates avoidable burden.

### Reconnect
Sometimes the problem is not excess but isolation. Give an underused element a natural relationship or consequence instead of inventing more lore.

## 6. Mystery bandwidth

Do not run every mystery at full activation.

Coordinate with `reader-model.md`:

- keep a small foreground set;
- let other questions cool;
- reactivate before payoff;
- transform old questions instead of endlessly stacking new ones.

A new mystery should often replace, deepen, or reframe an active question rather than merely join the pile.

## 7. Character-space allocation

Characters can feel alive without equal page time.

Allocate narrative space according to current function and lived importance, not democratic symmetry.

Minor characters may have independent lives that remain mostly offscreen. A brief appearance can imply depth through concrete work, relationships, history, and changed state without opening a full arc.

Do not promote every interesting side character into a POV.

## 8. Protecting ordinary life

Do not let pruning become total teleology.

Some details deserve to remain because they provide:

- baseline;
- social texture;
- humor;
- mortality;
- place memory;
- ordinary pleasure;
- evidence that life exceeds the plot.

The test is not "Does this fire later?" but "Does this earn the attention it receives?"

A one-line ordinary detail has low cost. A named subplot has high cost.

## 9. Complexity audit

For each crowded layer, ask:

- Which elements are active in reader memory now?
- Which can remain backstage?
- Which two elements perform the same structural job?
- Which named character could become a role without loss?
- Which faction exists only to provide one scene of opposition?
- Which mystery merely duplicates uncertainty already present?
- Which rule has not changed a decision?
- Which POV exists only to show information that could arrive another way?
- Can deeper edges replace additional nodes?
- What ordinary, non-plot life should remain after pruning?

### Remove-one test

Temporarily remove an element. If causality, character, world texture, thematic contrast, and reader experience remain essentially unchanged, merge, demote, or cut it.

### Complexity-yield test

Ask what new relationship, decision, interpretation, or pressure the added complexity enables. If the answer is only "more lore," the yield is low.

## 10. Structural originality audit

Complexity can still become repetitive when every arc uses the same skeleton with new names.

Strip surface decoration and compare recent arcs by:

- protagonist position;
- central relationship;
- social/institutional problem;
- trigger;
- hidden-cause shape;
- dominant decision pattern;
- escalation path;
- climax mechanism;
- closure pattern.

If several arcs reduce to the same sequence, change structure rather than nouns.

Do not ban tropes. Rotate the axes that produce causality.

Useful changes include:

- who possesses authority;
- who lacks information;
- whether conflict is private, institutional, economic, physical, or reputational;
- whether the protagonist is solving, enduring, negotiating, hiding, repairing, or choosing not to act;
- time scale;
- POV position;
- who benefits from continuation of the problem;
- whether the ending resolves, transforms, externalizes, or leaves bounded uncertainty.

### Reskin test

Remove names, powers, locations, and genre decoration from two arcs. If the remaining causal outline is effectively identical, redesign at least one higher-level axis.
