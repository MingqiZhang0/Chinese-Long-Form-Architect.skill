# Figurative Language and Image Systems

> SELECTABLE LANGUAGE CAPABILITY. Figurative density is not a proxy for literariness. Activate image/metaphor work only when it serves the current voice/poetics.

Use this reference when prose relies on metaphor, simile, personification, recurring imagery, or symbolic objects; when figurative language feels decorative, generic, mixed, or detached from viewpoint; or when a long novel needs an image system that evolves without becoming a codebook.

## Core principle

Figurative language should change perception, not merely announce that the prose is literary.

A useful figure may:

- make an unfamiliar relation graspable;
- reveal how a focalizer thinks;
- compress several concrete properties into one comparison;
- carry emotional contradiction without explanation;
- create a pattern whose meaning changes across time.

If removing the figure leaves the sentence clearer and equally vivid, the figure probably has no job.

## 1. Source-domain fit

A comparison should arise from a source domain available to the narrator, focalizer, or project voice.

Ask:

- What experiences has this person actually had?
- What professions, objects, landscapes, games, rituals, machines, foods, or social systems organize their thinking?
- Is the comparison something this consciousness would reach for under this pressure?
- Does the metaphor reveal the character, or only the author's vocabulary?

Do not give every viewpoint the same image reservoir.

A mechanic, archivist, child, soldier, botanist, and exhausted parent may all notice the same event but map it through different learned structures.

## 2. Metaphor as cognition

Prefer metaphors that preserve more than one relevant property.

A strong comparison may carry:

- shape;
- force;
- texture;
- timing;
- social relation;
- risk;
- direction;
- transformation.

A weak comparison often exists only because two things share one vague mood.

Do not use metaphor to replace missing observation. First know what the object/action actually does; then decide whether a comparison adds understanding.

## 3. Simile discipline

Repeated `like/as` or Chinese comparison markers can become an automatic sentence-finishing habit.

Before keeping a simile, ask:

- What exact feature is being compared?
- Is the source image more concrete than the target?
- Does the comparison fit the current viewpoint?
- Is the same emotional information already obvious from action/context?
- Has the paragraph already spent its strongest image?

Avoid chains in which every physical detail receives an immediate comparison. Let some objects remain materially themselves.

## 4. Image systems across long form

A recurring image becomes valuable when its **relation to the story changes**.

Possible evolution:

1. ordinary material detail;
2. private association;
3. shared or contested association;
4. altered meaning after an event;
5. return under new conditions;
6. final echo that carries accumulated history.

Do not declare the symbolic meaning in a state ledger merely because the image recurs. Use `formalization-boundary.md`: an image can accumulate resonance without having one canonical interpretation.

Track an image only when continuity requires remembering its material history, ownership, location, or explicit character association. Thematic meaning may remain in prose.

## 5. Extended metaphor

An extended metaphor needs internal discipline.

Once a source domain is established:

- keep later details compatible with that domain;
- do not switch to a second incompatible mechanism merely for freshness;
- stop before the comparison becomes a demonstration exercise;
- allow the literal scene to keep moving.

Extended metaphor works best when the source domain itself develops rather than when the prose keeps adding synonyms.

## 6. Cliche and dead metaphor

Not every familiar phrase is forbidden. Some conventional metaphors are ordinary language and should remain invisible when invisibility is useful.

Intervene when a phrase:

- pretends to be vivid but no longer creates an image;
- appears because the model reaches for a stock emotional sentence;
- contradicts the physical scene;
- creates register mismatch;
- repeats a familiar comparison cluster across unrelated characters.

Do not replace every conventional phrase with novelty. Forced originality is another form of artificial prose.

## 7. Density and negative space

Figurative density should vary with scene function, narrator, and pressure.

High density may fit:

- altered perception;
- lyrical reflection;
- sensory overload;
- a voice whose normal cognition is associative.

Low density may fit:

- procedural action;
- tactical urgency;
- emotionally defended dialogue;
- scenes where literal material detail already carries enough charge.

Do not add a metaphor to every paragraph merely to maintain a literary texture.

Use `restraint-negative-space.md` when the real problem is over-explanation or page density rather than the figure itself.

## 8. Chinese prose considerations

Chinese figurative prose can become artificial through repeated four-character cadence, symmetrical parallelism, idiom stacking, or short `X like Y` closures.

Keep separate:

- lexicalized/fixed expressions;
- idioms with conventional syntax and register;
- live metaphor created in the current sentence;
- recurring project imagery.

Use `chinese-grammar-idiom.md` for idiom correctness and phraseological naturalness. A correct idiom may still be the wrong figurative choice for the speaker, focalizer, or scene.

Do not assume that compressing a comparison into a four-character phrase makes it more literary.

## 9. Audit

For a passage with noticeable figurative language, ask:

- What does each figure let the reader perceive that literal wording would not?
- Whose mind or voice owns this comparison?
- Does the source domain belong to that person or narrator?
- Are several figures competing in the same sentence/paragraph?
- Does one strong image already do the work of the weaker ones?
- Is an image recurring because its meaning evolved, or because the system likes it?
- Is the prose explaining the metaphor after using it?
- Could a concrete material detail carry more force here?
- Has every paragraph begun to end with a comparison?

### Source-domain swap test

Replace the comparison mentally with another equally generic image. If nothing about character, setting, or meaning changes, the original was probably decorative.

### Literal survival test

Remove the metaphor. If the scene becomes clearer and loses nothing important, cut or rebuild the figure.

### Viewpoint ownership test

Ask whether another major character would naturally produce the same comparison. If yes, confirm that this is project/narrator voice rather than accidental homogenization.

## 10. Failure modes

### Ornament reflex

Every emotional beat receives a simile or metaphor.

Repair: preserve the strongest image and let literal action carry the rest.

### Imported metaphor

The figure comes from knowledge or experience the focalizer does not possess.

Repair: move the comparison into the established narrator voice or rebuild it from the character's source domains.

### Mixed mechanism

One figure begins as machinery and ends as weather, anatomy, or architecture without purposeful transformation.

Repair: choose one controlling relation or separate the figures.

### Symbolic overcommitment

A recurring object is assigned one official thematic meaning and later scenes are forced to serve it.

Repair: preserve material continuity while allowing interpretive meaning to remain plural or unstable.

### Cliche panic

Normal language is aggressively replaced by conspicuous novelty.

Repair: value precision and voice over originality for its own sake.

### Model-image fingerprint

Unrelated characters repeatedly use the same image families, sentence shapes, and comparison cadence.

Repair: audit source domains and voice ownership; remove figures that belong to the model rather than the story.
