# Social Transmission, Rumor, and Reputation

Use this reference when information travels through communities, institutions, media, gossip, testimony, public memory, or legend, especially when the transmitted version begins changing behavior. Treat it as a selectable social-causality capability: do not manufacture rumor, reputation systems, or public versions in projects whose current causal cone or Project Poetics does not need them.

## Core principle

Information does not merely travel. It is selected, compressed, interpreted, retold, recorded, denied, weaponized, forgotten, and sometimes converted into social reality.

Model important chains as:

> event -> perception -> memory -> telling -> channel -> retelling -> public version -> behavior -> feedback

Do not assume the version circulating later is a degraded copy of one pristine message. Each stage has motives, limits, and schemas.

## 1. Keep versions distinct

Where relevant, distinguish:

- objective event;
- what a witness perceived;
- what the witness remembers;
- what the witness chooses to say;
- what a listener understands;
- what gets repeated;
- official record;
- public rumor;
- reputation;
- later myth or simplified historical version.

These may diverge without anyone consciously lying.

## 2. Common transformation mechanisms

Historical rumor and memory research often describes three useful tendencies:

- **leveling** - details disappear and the account becomes shorter or simpler;
- **sharpening** - selected details become disproportionately memorable or emphasized;
- **assimilation** - the account shifts toward existing expectations, group beliefs, motives, or familiar story shapes.

Use these as reasoning tools, not mandatory stages.

Additional forces include:

- prestige of the teller;
- repetition frequency;
- emotional salience;
- shame and face-saving;
- group identity;
- political or economic incentive;
- medium constraints;
- screenshot, edit, clipping, headline, or algorithmic amplification;
- official confirmation or denial;
- legal risk;
- audience expectations;
- whether the version is easy to retell.

## 3. Source chains and independence

When several people repeat the same claim, ask whether they are independent sources or descendants of one original account.

A hundred repetitions of one contaminated source do not create a hundred pieces of evidence.

Track when useful:

- first known source;
- direct witness versus hearsay;
- transmission links;
- medium;
- known editing or incentives;
- whether two versions share distinctive wording or error;
- what each teller could independently know.

Use `knowledge-topology.md` for investigative reasoning.

## 4. Medium changes the story

The same event told through:

- family conversation;
- group chat;
- local news;
- police report;
- workplace memo;
- religious sermon;
- short video;
- anonymous post;
- schoolyard rumor;
- official history;

will preserve different details and encourage different interpretations.

Do not flatten all transmission into generic gossip.

## 5. Reputation as a causal state

Once a circulating version changes who gets:

- hired;
- invited;
- trusted;
- mocked;
- followed;
- feared;
- paid;
- investigated;
- obeyed;
- blamed;

reputation has become a real social state even if the underlying claim is false.

Correction may change belief before it changes behavior, records, jokes, alliances, prices, or risk assessments. Pair with `state-inertia.md`.

## 6. Rumor can generate new evidence-like effects

A rumor may cause:

- witnesses to reinterpret memory;
- copycat behavior;
- people to avoid a place, making it seem abandoned;
- officials to investigate, creating records;
- enemies to change strategy;
- opportunists to exploit the story;
- believers to produce ritual or material traces;
- later observers to mistake consequences of the rumor for evidence of the original claim.

This is not retroactive proof. It is feedback.

## 7. Corrections and counter-rumor

A correction succeeds only if it can compete on the actual network.

Ask:

- does the correct source reach the same people?
- is it as memorable?
- does accepting it cost status or identity?
- does it explain the same anxiety?
- who repeats it?
- is the old version more useful to anyone?

Do not keep false beliefs alive merely to preserve plot difficulty. Strong evidence, trusted sources, or changed incentives can update people quickly.

## 8. Legend formation

Long transmission can compress a complex event into a stable story kernel.

A useful long-term progression is not mandatory but may resemble:

- specific event;
- memorable retelling;
- simplified local version;
- moralized or identity-serving version;
- ritualized, commemorated, commercialized, or politicized version;
- later generation treating the version as inherited common sense.

Preserve some incompatible versions when social groups have different stakes.

## 9. Do not make every rumor plot-relevant

Ordinary misinformation, gossip, jokes, reputational drift, and local stories may exist without hiding a clue.

A living social field contains excess speech.

## Audit

- What was actually observed?
- Who first told whom?
- What was dropped, emphasized, or assimilated?
- Which channels carry which version?
- Are apparent independent sources really independent?
- What behavior changed because of the circulating version?
- What remains after correction?
- Does the rumor have social life beyond serving the protagonist's mystery?
