# Environment and Object Description

Use this reference when places feel thin, scenery is reduced to mood/metaphor, action seems to occur in empty space, a concrete object deserves sustained attention, or large-scale narration lacks physical reality.

## Core principle

Description is an allocation of attention. Its job depends on the current Project Poetics: orientation, affordance, social/material life, familiarity, atmosphere, perception, memory, contrast, evidence, spectacle, or simple concrete presence may all be legitimate.

Do not equate descriptive value with scenery quantity, symbolism, or plot utility. A place/object may deserve direct attention without becoming setup/payoff. Conversely, a causally important environment may be described sparsely when the reader already knows it.

Use descriptive resolution according to **salience and reader experience**, not a fixed rule that higher causal change always deserves more description.

## 1. Distinguish four descriptive jobs

A passage may do one or more of these:

- **orientation** - establish where things are in relation to each other;
- **inhabitation** - make the environment feel continuously lived in or physically active;
- **focal description** - stay with one object, surface, landscape, body, structure, or phenomenon long enough for the reader to see it;
- **interpretation** - show what the viewpoint concludes, remembers, fears, or values about what is perceived.

Do not collapse all four into one adjective or one simile.

## 2. Choose a descriptive resolution

Read the current Project Poetics first. Resolution may rise because a place must be navigated, because atmosphere/familiarity is itself the experience, because perception is reorganizing, because an object carries use-history, or because the scene is a set piece. It may remain low even during a major plot event if description is not the active reader task.

Do not describe every place at equal depth. Before drafting, decide roughly how much visual/material commitment the moment deserves.

### Resolution 0 - name only

Use when location is already known or materially irrelevant.

> He crossed the canteen and went upstairs.

### Resolution 1 - anchor

Give two or three cues sufficient for orientation or mood.

> The canteen had already closed half its shutters. Steam still hung over the tray return, and someone was dragging chairs across the tile near the entrance.

### Resolution 2 - focal object or local field

Stay long enough to establish literal form, material, wear, use, and relation to nearby space.

Use for objects that will be handled, remembered, damaged, feared, repaired, or later altered.

### Resolution 3 - sustained environment

Let the place occupy several paragraphs because scale, dread, beauty, grief, social texture, or tactical understanding depends on duration.

### Resolution 4 - set-piece landscape or spectacle

Use rarely. Establish scale, distribution, light, weather, material behavior, and slow background movement before reducing the scene to action or abstraction.

These are not formal levels to mention in prose. They are a drafting control against two opposite failures: under-description and indiscriminate over-description.

### Resolution is moment-specific, not place-specific

The same room may deserve Resolution 3 on first arrival, Resolution 0 during a later routine crossing, Resolution 2 when a damaged window becomes tactically important, and Resolution 3 again after a fire changes the whole space.

Do not decide that a location is inherently a "high-description place." Decide what the current narrative moment requires the reader to perceive.

## 2A. Raise or lower descriptive resolution deliberately

Raise resolution when one or more of these are true:

- the reader needs a usable spatial model for later movement, danger, pursuit, combat, search, or concealment;
- the place or object is new, radically changed, socially revealing, or difficult to imagine from a name alone;
- later damage, disappearance, return, or transformation will matter and therefore needs a baseline;
- scale, beauty, disgust, grief, dread, wonder, boredom, waiting, or intimacy requires the prose to spend experiential time;
- a material clue, anomaly, object, body, machine, or piece of infrastructure deserves close observation;
- the environment itself constrains choice and is therefore part of causality;
- the viewpoint's profession, memory, fear, desire, or task makes a particular region genuinely attention-worthy.

Lower resolution when:

- the place is already established and materially unchanged;
- the passage is only carrying the reader between two consequential moments;
- the current action is urgent enough that the viewpoint would not inspect the environment;
- extra detail would repeat an already usable spatial model rather than update it;
- the object has no causal, experiential, social, or character value beyond being present;
- the narrative needs velocity more than perceptual duration.

Do not confuse high importance with high word count. A decisive object may deserve one exact sentence rather than a paragraph. Do not confuse low plot importance with zero descriptive value; ordinary physical detail can still create inhabitation and trust.

### Mention, anchor, dwell, or inspect

Use **mention** when a name or category is enough: the reader already knows the place, no spatial decision depends on it, and the moment should keep moving.

Use **anchor** when the reader needs immediate orientation: one dominant spatial relation plus one or two concrete cues.

Use **dwell** when duration itself is part of the experience: arrival, awe, grief, aftermath, waiting, social observation, landscape, architectural scale, or a changed familiar place.

Use **inspection** when attention has a task: search, repair, diagnosis, evidence, threat assessment, craft, appraisal, recognition, or comparison. Inspection should follow the task's logic rather than generic visual completeness.

## 2B. Begin with gist by default, then spend attention selectively

In an unfamiliar scene, the reader usually benefits from a coarse whole before disconnected close-ups: what kind of place this is, its broad spatial envelope, dominant material/light, navigability, crowding, depth, or scale. Then move toward the few regions that matter.

This is a default, not a commandment. Begin with a close detail when disorientation, concealment, intimacy, shock, waking, injury, restricted vision, or delayed recognition is itself the intended experience. If you begin local, eventually provide enough larger structure for the reader to understand where the detail belongs.

Do not describe what is visually loud merely because it is visually loud. In lived perception, goals and meaning often determine attention more strongly than raw brightness, color, or contrast. Let the viewpoint's current task answer: **what deserves processing now?**

## 2C. Treat descriptive order as attention choreography

There is no universal natural rule that prose must scan left-to-right, top-to-bottom, or near-to-far. Choose an order because the spatial relation, viewpoint, movement, revelation, or emotional effect benefits from it.

Useful patterns include:

- **global -> local**: establish the field, then isolate a meaningful figure or anomaly; strong default for unfamiliar environments;
- **local -> global**: begin with a hand, stain, sound, footprint, crack, face, or object and widen out when delayed recognition or intimacy matters;
- **near -> far**: useful on entry, reconnaissance, travel, pursuit, battlefield orientation, and any scene where depth or route matters;
- **far -> near**: useful when a distant threat, procession, weather front, vehicle, sound source, or approaching person progressively acquires consequence;
- **bottom -> top**: useful for monumentality, ascent, tall bodies/structures, cliffs, towers, machines, vertical social hierarchy made physical, or when the viewpoint first meets the base/contact point and then follows height;
- **top -> bottom**: useful for descending danger, ceilings/sky/upper stories exerting pressure, waterfalls, falling structures, aerial viewpoints, or when height must be understood before what lies beneath it;
- **path-led**: reveal space in the order a body can actually traverse it; often strongest for corridors, alleys, caves, houses, stations, ships, mazes, or unfamiliar interiors;
- **figure -> ground**: begin with the person/object that matters and expand only enough environment to explain its placement, isolation, vulnerability, or incongruity;
- **ground -> figure**: establish normal spatial baseline, then let an anomaly become legible against it; especially useful for clues, horror, social observation, and changed familiar places;
- **orbit / relational inspection**: move around an object or cluster by functional relation - hinge to lock to frame, wound to clothing to floor, machine housing to cable to power source - rather than arbitrary visual direction.

Do not name these patterns in prose. They are planning tools.


### Paragraphize by attention path, not object count

Keep a descriptive sequence in one semantic paragraph while one viewpoint task, perceptual anchor, or attention path still governs it. Do not break merely because attention touches another object. Break when spatial scale, perceptual task, cognitive meaning, or scene function reorganizes enough that the reader benefits from a new local model.

For paragraph boundary strength, bridge paragraphs, place-memory re-entry, and display-safe breaks, use `paragraph-architecture.md`.

### Direction must earn itself

Use a directional scan when direction carries information: height, depth, route, approach, concealment, access, hierarchy, scale, movement, or reveal.

Use a flatter distributed field when simultaneity matters more than route: a cafeteria at lunch, a square before a protest, an office at closing time, a battlefield before motion begins, a market, a workshop, or a familiar room whose social occupancy matters more than geometric discovery. In a flat field, do not inventory everything. Select a few distributed anchors that imply the whole.

A directional description should create a mental path. A flat description should create a usable field. Both fail if they become a catalogue.

## 2D. Re-enter by delta, not by reset

When returning to an established place, do not automatically replay its establishing description. Ask what changed since the last meaningful view:

- light, season, weather, crowd, smell, maintenance, damage, furniture, signage, price, security, ownership, noise, routine;
- what is missing;
- what has been repaired badly or replaced;
- what the viewpoint now notices because the viewpoint has changed.

If almost nothing changed and orientation remains secure, a name plus one fresh anchor may be enough. If the place has changed profoundly, describe the **difference structure**: retain a few old landmarks so the reader can feel the transformation rather than receiving a wholly new room.

### Establish before you destroy when contrast matters

If a later scene depends on the emotional or tactical meaning of destruction, evacuation, abandonment, pollution, renovation, or occupation, establish enough of the earlier normal state for the change to have an object. Do not over-describe every future casualty merely to manufacture poignancy; establish baseline only where later contrast genuinely matters.

## 3. Literal-object pass

When an object deserves attention, first render the object itself.

Possible concrete dimensions:

- shape, size, proportion;
- color variation rather than a single label;
- material and construction;
- surface: seams, dents, rust, stains, condensation, dust, polish, cracks, grease, fabric nap, chipped paint;
- light: direction, reflection, glare, shadow, translucence;
- relation to floor, wall, horizon, nearby objects, or body;
- motion or stillness;
- sound or vibration;
- temperature, pressure, humidity, smell, airflow when relevant;
- evidence of use, repair, maintenance, neglect, ownership, age, or class.

Do not force every dimension into the same paragraph. Select the few that make this object materially specific.

### Example: direct description before interpretation

Weak:

> The old iron door looked like a mouth that had forgotten how to open.

Material first:

> The door had once been painted a dull green. Most of the lower half had blistered away from the metal, and three brown rust tracks ran straight down from the hinge plates. The key had polished an oval of bare steel around the lock. Someone had stuffed a blackened twist of cotton into the upper corner of the frame to stop the winter draft.

A metaphor may still follow. The object no longer depends on it to exist.

## 4. Commit after you zoom in

A common failure is:

> object appears -> one vivid comparison -> narration immediately leaves.

This produces flashes, not environments.

Once the prose deliberately zooms into a focal object, remain with it long enough to establish at least a small cluster of literal observations, relations, or changes. Do not escape immediately into symbolism, explanation, or dialogue.

“Commit” does not mean write a catalogue. It means give the reader time to finish seeing what the narration asked them to look at.

## 5. Allow direct description

Do not turn “integrate description with action” into another rigid rule. Sometimes the strongest choice is simply to stop and describe the street, machine, landscape, room, body, weather system, or object directly.

The viewpoint does not need to touch an object, remember childhood, or discover a clue in order for the narration to look at it. A descriptive paragraph can justify itself through perception, scale, beauty, dread, orientation, material curiosity, or the need to establish a normal baseline.

Use action to reveal physical properties when action is genuinely occurring. Do not invent busywork merely to smuggle in description.

Likewise, do not interrupt every sustained description with dialogue because of fear that the prose will “stop.” Sometimes stopping is the point.

## 6. Build spatial continuity

For unfamiliar, large, dangerous, or tactically important places, privately establish a usable field. In persistent projects, treat `spatiotemporal-scene-state.md` as the canonical source for geometry; this module decides what portion of that geometry reaches prose.

Useful anchors:

- ground/floor or supporting surface;
- overhead space/sky/ceiling;
- nearest dominant object;
- far boundary/horizon;
- main route of movement;
- light source;
- foreground, middle distance, background;
- relevant exits, barriers, elevation, or blind zones.

Do not dump a map. Re-anchor when movement, attention, danger, or scale makes it necessary.

A reader should not have to reconstruct a room from disconnected close-ups every time someone moves.

## 7. Let the environment persist

Once established, a place should not disappear when dialogue starts.

Let selected background processes continue:

- machinery cycling;
- changing light;
- weather reaching the scene;
- traffic, footsteps, public announcements;
- irrigation, ventilation, steam, dust, rainwater, leaves, animals;
- distant work, construction, cleaning, screens, elevators;
- characters touching, stepping around, dirtying, opening, closing, leaning on, repairing, or waiting beside objects.

The point is not scenic garnish between every two lines of dialogue. The point is to preserve the sense that the world has not paused to listen to the conversation.

## 8. Use environmental time

Not every descriptive sentence must advance plot.

When scale, grief, exhaustion, awe, dread, boredom, waiting, or ordinary life benefits from duration, allow the prose to remain with the place while nothing decisive happens.

A cloud shadow may cross a field. Water may continue dripping. A train may take time to arrive. A distant structure may shed fragments. Workers may keep stacking crates while two characters fail to say what they came to say.

Duration is not automatically filler. It can establish reality, scale, emotional pressure, and contrast.

## 8A. Make description occur inside events when the scene is kinetic

For collisions, accidents, pursuits, falls, attacks, crowd disturbances, mechanical failures, public interruptions, or any scene whose vividness depends on bodies and systems moving through space, do not treat description as a static layer laid behind action. Let movement reveal the environment and let the environment redirect movement.

Use `references/dynamic-vividness.md` for micro-causal chains, situation-model updates, affordances, embodied geometry, occlusion, stress-limited perception, independent foreground/background processes, crowd heterogeneity, response latency, cross-thread collisions, and aftermath propagation.

Keep only the core rule here: **kinetic vividness comes from legible state changes inside an independently moving world, not from maximal detail or frame-by-frame choreography.**

## 9. Match description to viewpoint

Description is world filtered through attention.

An engineer may notice repairs, load paths, access panels, mismatched components, drainage, unsafe improvisation, or maintenance shortcuts.

A nurse may notice smell, cleanliness, posture, queue flow, skin color, fatigue, accessibility, and who has been waiting too long.

A child may notice height, forbidden areas, food, bright surfaces, hiding places, and adults behaving strangely.

A commuter may omit what a visitor finds spectacular because repetition has made it invisible.

A frightened person may perceive only a narrow physical corridor. A bored person may notice trivial defects precisely because nothing else demands attention.

Do not make every viewpoint describe with the same generic literary camera.

## 10. Use sensory channels by relevance, not quota

Vision dominates easily, but bodily reality often comes from another channel:

- vibration through a railing;
- dry cold inside the nose;
- hot plastic and dust in a bus;
- wet soil after irrigation;
- fan noise masked by rain;
- a tool's texture through gloves;
- pressure change in an enclosed chamber;
- detergent, frying oil, ozone, rust, old cloth, concrete after rain.

Do not insert one item from every sense. Choose what the body would actually notice.

## 11. Avoid adjective summaries in place of perception

Words such as old, warm, eerie, luxurious, filthy, oppressive, futuristic, devastated, sacred, beautiful, or uncanny are conclusions.

They may be useful after evidence. They are weak substitutes for evidence when the scene requires embodiment.

Ask:

> What would the reader have to see, hear, touch, smell, or infer socially in order to reach that conclusion without being told?

## 12. Use metaphor after perception, not instead of perception

Metaphor should add a new relation or emotional angle. It should not perform basic visual labor.

Watch for habitual anthropomorphism:

- mountains crouching;
- buildings staring;
- clouds devouring;
- doors becoming mouths;
- streets becoming veins;
- machinery breathing.

These can work occasionally. Repetition turns the world into one narrator's metaphor machine.

A useful test:

> Remove every simile and metaphor from the paragraph. Can the reader still see the object or place?

If not, literal embodiment is too weak.

## 13. Let some objects remain only objects

Not every detail should symbolize theme, foreshadow danger, prove worldbuilding, or later become a clue.

A badly placed vending machine, repaired curb, faded sticker, loose ceiling panel, condensation on a pipe, scratched table edge, bent bicycle basket, or tree blocking a balcony may simply be there.

Ordinary detail creates trust. Later abnormality becomes stronger because it disturbs a world that had independent material existence before the plot arrived.

## 14. Show use and maintenance

Material reality becomes especially convincing when objects bear human history without explanation.

Look for:

- repair patches;
- replaced parts of slightly different color;
- unofficial labels;
- worn handles;
- polished edges from repeated touch;
- cables rerouted around later construction;
- paint covering older paint;
- furniture positioned to compensate for a defect;
- habits built around infrastructure limitations.

This is often more effective worldbuilding than a paragraph explaining how old the district, machine, institution, or technology is.

## 15. Macro scale and landscape

For cosmic, aerial, planetary, continental, architectural, or mass-crowd scenes, avoid permanent abstraction.

Before reducing the landscape to data or metaphor, establish some combination of:

- overall geometry or extent;
- a scale anchor the reader already understands;
- distribution of major forms rather than one giant noun;
- material differences across the field;
- light and atmospheric depth;
- slow motion visible only at that scale: cloud systems, traffic streams, shadow, smoke, tides, rotating structures, migration, weather fronts;
- one or two local details that prove the vast scene is physically inhabited.

“Three thousand kilometers of flesh” is scale information. It becomes landscape only when the reader can begin to perceive how that mass occupies the world.

## 16. Hard cuts from macro to human

A project may cut suddenly from cosmic/civilizational/planetary scale to one specific person, room, body, task, meal, injury, fear, or inconvenience without an explanatory bridge.

Use the cut when it changes scale, social meaning, knowledge, emotional weight, or the reader's understanding of what the macro event means locally.

The destination viewpoint must have local reality independent of reacting to spectacle. Do not cut to an anonymous person only so they can look up and say how amazing or terrifying the event is.

Do not establish a fixed macro -> human -> macro -> human rhythm. Surprise comes partly from not knowing where the narrative will land next.

A sudden cut may be narratively abrupt while remaining causally honest.

## 17. Scene-entry pass

When entering a new, changed, spectacular, tactically important, emotionally important, or recurring place, privately check:

- Can the reader locate ground, height, near, and far if needed?
- What is the dominant light?
- What major material occupies the scene?
- What is the air/weather/system doing?
- What continues moving without the protagonist?
- Is there one object worth closer resolution?
- Does the reader need a field, a path, or a close inspection?
- Should attention move global->local, local->global, near->far, far->near, vertically, along a route, or remain distributed? Why?
- Is this a first entry, a routine re-entry, or a changed-place comparison?
- What would this viewpoint notice that another would ignore?
- Am I naming the mood, or giving the evidence that creates it?

Do not answer all questions on the page. Use them to prevent empty-space scenes.

## 18. Revision tests

### Literal-removal test

Remove metaphors and evaluative adjectives from a descriptive paragraph. If almost nothing perceptible remains, rebuild literal form/material/spatial relation.

### Map test

After a substantial action scene, can the reader roughly say where the main bodies/objects are and how movement changed that relation? If not, re-anchor.

### Persistence test

Read only the dialogue and action after the establishing paragraph. Does the location vanish completely? If yes, restore selective environmental continuity.

### Resolution test

Did the prose spend half a page on a disposable cup but summarize the first view of a continent in one generic sentence? Reallocate attention according to narrative and perceptual importance.

### Attention-order test

Can you explain why the description moves in its chosen order? If the answer is only "because top-to-bottom/left-to-right is orderly," choose an order tied to gist, task, movement, scale, figure-ground relation, or reveal.

### Gist test

In an unfamiliar place, does the reader receive enough coarse structure to place later details? If not, add a broad anchor unless deliberate disorientation is the effect.

### Delta re-entry test

On returning to a known place, is the prose describing the whole place again or showing what changed, persisted, disappeared, or now matters differently? Prefer delta unless full re-establishment is necessary.

### Commitment test

Whenever the prose deliberately says “look at this,” ask whether it stayed long enough for the reader to finish looking.

### Plot-ownership test

Count prominent scenic details in a scene. If nearly all are clues, symbols, callbacks, or setup, add some unowned ordinary reality or remove the feeling of design.

### Viewpoint test

Could the same description be pasted under another viewpoint with no change? If yes, adjust attention rather than merely vocabulary.

### Scale-cut test

For each macro-to-human cut, ask whether the human scene has its own local objective, task, relationship, body, or inconvenience. If it exists only to react to scale, the cut is decorative.
