# High-Pressure Institutional Negotiation

> SELECTABLE SCENE CAPABILITY. Load for councils, hearings, boards, treaties, succession, war rooms, and other procedural negotiation when relevant.

Use this reference for councils, hearings, court politics, sect assemblies, boardrooms, war rooms, treaty talks, interrogations with formal stakes, succession disputes, and other scenes where characters fight through authority, procedure, resources, legitimacy, information, status, or dependency rather than immediate violence.

If the user asks for a meeting or negotiation with a *Dune-like* structural feel, translate that request into the principles below: ceremonial or institutional pressure, asymmetrical power, layered motives, costly speech, information asymmetry, resource dependency, and latent coercion. Do not imitate Frank Herbert's diction, sentence rhythm, metaphors, dialogue phrasing, or scene choreography.

## 1. Build the power map before writing dialogue

For every consequential participant, know privately:

- formal role and jurisdiction;
- actual objective;
- public objective they can defend;
- authority they can exercise without permission;
- authority they can only request, recommend, veto, delay, or trigger;
- resources, records, appointments, votes, access, expertise, force, or legitimacy they control;
- dependencies and vulnerabilities;
- outside options if the meeting fails;
- time pressure;
- reputational constraints;
- red lines;
- what they know, suspect, and cannot verify;
- what they think the other participants know;
- what they cannot safely say in this room.

Do not begin with clever lines and reverse-engineer motives afterward.

## 2. Treat the room as a power diagram

Institutional space is not neutral. Decide which details materially express power:

- who convened the meeting;
- who chose the room;
- who sits, stands, waits, or enters last;
- fixed versus improvised seating;
- who is entitled to speak without invitation;
- who may interrupt whom;
- titles and forms of address;
- who controls the agenda;
- who controls the record or minutes;
- which documents are physically present;
- who may call witnesses, request files, or verify claims;
- whether the discussion is informal, privileged, public, recorded, archived, or binding.

Use only the details that affect the scene. Do not turn every chair into symbolism.

## 3. Fight first over what the issue *is*

The strongest political move is often framing.

Participants may try to define the same event as:

- a resource-efficiency question;
- a disciplinary matter;
- a succession dispute;
- a safety review;
- a private disagreement;
- a precedent-setting constitutional issue;
- an emergency;
- a routine administrative correction.

The chosen frame determines:

- who has jurisdiction;
- what evidence matters;
- what procedure applies;
- who must recuse;
- what threshold is required;
- what remedies become available;
- what enters the permanent record.

Do not let framing remain decorative rhetoric. It must change available actions.

## 4. Give every consequential line at least one function

A serious negotiation line should usually do one or more of the following:

- obtain information;
- test a belief;
- force a public commitment;
- preserve deniability;
- narrow or widen the issue;
- change the burden of proof;
- invoke precedent;
- create a record;
- delay;
- accelerate;
- split a coalition;
- protect face;
- threaten indirectly through a lawful option;
- trade one concession for another;
- establish jurisdiction;
- reserve future rights;
- make refusal costly;
- move from informal discussion to binding procedure.

If a line merely sounds intelligent but changes nothing, cut or simplify it.

## 5. Separate three layers of speech

For important lines, distinguish privately:

1. **literal content** - what was actually said;
2. **public institutional meaning** - what the room can officially treat the statement as meaning;
3. **private strategic meaning** - what informed participants infer from timing, wording, omissions, or context.

Do not narrate all three layers after every line. Let readers infer private meaning when the evidence is available.

## 6. Make wording costly

In high-stakes rooms, wording matters because statements can become:

- admissions;
- promises;
- precedents;
- grounds for recusal;
- grounds for sanction;
- evidence of bias;
- commitments that allies later enforce;
- quoted records.

Characters should sometimes answer a narrower question than the one asked, refuse a premise, ask for the wording to be entered into the record, or insist on defining a term before agreeing.

Do not make everyone speak in riddles. Precision can be more dangerous than obscurity.

## 7. Use procedure as action

Procedural moves are not filler when they change the option set. Useful moves include:

- request a record;
- call for verification;
- move to formal session;
- ask that a statement be entered into the record;
- demand recusal;
- challenge jurisdiction;
- invoke precedent;
- postpone pending evidence;
- call a vote;
- force roll-call responsibility;
- narrow the motion;
- split one motion into several;
- condition approval;
- freeze assets or permissions temporarily;
- refer the issue to another office;
- require written grounds;
- declare an emergency exception;
- refuse to recognize an informal promise as binding.

A procedural move becomes dramatic only after the reader understands its consequence.

## 8. Use asymmetry, not omniscience

Nobody should know the whole game.

Pressure comes from different actors controlling different pieces:

- one has the legal authority but poor information;
- one has evidence but no jurisdiction;
- one controls resources but needs legitimacy;
- one has force but cannot use it without political cost;
- one has status but no formal vote;
- one has a veto but fears being blamed for delay.

Do not create one master strategist who secretly understands every motive unless the story has earned that capacity.

## 9. Let silence have a concrete cause

Silence may mean:

- waiting for the chair to recognize a speaker;
- refusing to dignify a premise;
- calculating whether a statement will enter the record;
- uncertainty;
- fear of precedent;
- allowing an ally to take responsibility;
- testing whether an opponent will overcommit;
- protecting face.

Do not write generic “the room fell into a terrifying silence” after every strong line.

## 10. Build escalation in discrete steps

Do not sustain maximum tension from the first sentence.

A high-pressure meeting often escalates by changing the institutional state:

1. routine report or narrow dispute;
2. challenge to an assumption;
3. request for evidence or record;
4. reframing of the issue;
5. forced public position;
6. procedural escalation;
7. concession, ruling, vote, adjournment, referral, or deadlock;
8. aftermath in which participants reinterpret what just became possible.

This is not a required beat sheet. Use only the steps the situation generates.

## 11. Make concessions asymmetric

A concession need not mean weakness.

Characters may concede:

- money to protect jurisdiction;
- face to protect secrecy;
- timing to protect precedent;
- a minor investigation to block a deeper one;
- a symbolic apology to keep resources;
- a temporary measure to prevent permanent transfer.

Ask what each side values least among the things the other side values most.

Good negotiations often end with everyone losing something different.

## 12. Keep latent force present but usually unused

In fantasy, military, criminal, or authoritarian settings, participants may possess overwhelming physical force. Do not make every disagreement a threat display.

Force matters most when the room knows:

- who could use it;
- what would legally or politically justify it;
- what using it would cost;
- which institutions would react;
- whether coercion would destroy the objective it was meant to secure.

A powerful character can be constrained precisely because their goal requires the institution to remain intact.

## 13. Let status appear through permissions

Show hierarchy through what people are allowed to do, not repeated narrator labels.

Examples:

- one participant can speak without being recognized;
- another must route questions through the chair;
- one can request a file and receive it immediately;
- another can only petition for access;
- someone addresses an equal by title after the discussion becomes formal;
- the chair can end one line of argument by changing the agenda.

Permission structures make status tangible.

## 14. Avoid fake sophistication

Reject these patterns:

- every line is a polished aphorism;
- everyone instantly understands every hidden implication;
- antagonists openly confess motives they need to conceal;
- narrator explains the subtext after each line;
- every silence is ominous;
- every participant is equally strategic;
- one clever comeback decides a structural conflict;
- insults substitute for leverage;
- people threaten actions they cannot actually take;
- the meeting exists only so the protagonist can humiliate a room.

Competent politics is often bureaucratic, partial, frustrating, and unfinished.

## 15. End on a state change, not necessarily a victory

A meeting can end with:

- a motion formally entered;
- an investigation authorized or blocked;
- a record requested;
- a vote delayed;
- a coalition exposed;
- a participant forced onto the record;
- temporary rights preserved at a new cost;
- an issue transferred to another jurisdiction;
- a compromise whose future danger is now visible;
- no agreement, but a narrower set of future options.

The strongest ending is often a quiet procedural sentence whose consequence the reader now understands.

## 16. Negotiation scene audit

Before finalizing, ask:

- What does each major participant want *today*?
- What can each participant actually do if refused?
- Who controls the agenda, record, verification, timing, and enforcement?
- What issue definition favors each side?
- Which statement creates the greatest future liability?
- Which silence has a concrete reason?
- What changed in the option set during the scene?
- Which concession bought what?
- Could any participant obtain their objective more cheaply outside this meeting?
- Did the scene rely on insults or intelligence theatre instead of leverage?
- Did the narrator explain subtext the reader could infer?
- Does the exit state create political consequences beyond the room?
