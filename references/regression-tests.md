## Selection rule

- Do not run the whole catalog for every chapter.
- During Skill maintenance, test execution depth is owned by `test-tier-contract.md`; this catalog remains a symptom/owner lookup, not a second tier list.
- Search by failure symptom or owner module.
- Legacy numeric IDs are stable lookup aids only; gaps mark merged historical duplicates.
- Repair the owner-level cause rather than adding another local rule.

## A. Foundations, causality, continuity, and scene selection

| ID | Failure family | Fail if | Pass if |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Deus ex machina rescue | A trapped protagonist is saved by an ally, route, technology, legal immunity, or power that had no prior existence or causal support. | At least one earlier foothold made the rescue possible, and the rescuer had a credible reason, means, timing, and knowledge to act. |
| 2 | Fake genius | The protagonist predicts a plan because every opponent ignores obvious alternatives. | Opponent options were genuinely constrained by incentives, information, law, time, reputation, resources, or competing risks. |
| 3 | Exposition dialogue | Two experts explain basic shared knowledge to each other for the reader. | They discuss the present implication, disagreement, exception, or consequence of the shared rule instead. |
| 4 | Fluorescent foreshadowing | A minor object is isolated with ominous narration solely so the reader knows it will matter later. | The detail has an immediate local reason to appear and can remain natural before its later significance is known. |
| 5 | Mechanical Chekhov rule | Every named object must later become plot-critical. | Only details granted narrative pressure create promises. Ordinary details are allowed to remain ordinary. |
| 6 | Retcon disguised as reveal | A new truth directly contradicts what prior objective narration established. | Earlier text remains true under the new interpretation, or the change is explicitly handled as a retcon through change control. |
| 7 | Progression exception | A new ability appears exactly when needed and violates earlier limits. | The capability follows from established principles, has a prior possibility space, preserves costs or explains their transformation, and creates new counterplay or consequences. |
| 8 | World freezes around protagonist | Institutions, side characters, shops, jobs, investigations, markets, and relationships only move when the protagonist observes them. Variant (legacy Test 75): Removing the protagonist for weeks or months causes important people, organizations, romances, markets, investigations, and threats to freeze. | Off-page processes continue and later produce plausible consequences. A remove-the-protagonist test produces autonomous change, including some developments that help, hurt, or simply bypass the protagonist. |
| 9 | Checklist daily life | A meal, commute, laundry scene, or weather paragraph exists mainly to prove that the story has life texture. | Ordinary detail follows from class, schedule, place, habit, infrastructure, relationship, or current objective and does not call attention to its own realism. |
| 10 | AI literary veneer | Every quiet scene contains polished aphorisms, decorative metaphors, symmetrical paragraphs, and explicit emotional conclusions. | Most prose remains transparent; strong language is selective; concrete social and sensory reality carries meaning without constant commentary. |
| 11 | Battle spectacle without emotion | The fight is technically elaborate but the reader has no grounded reason to care about the outcome. | Prior attachment, objectives, bodily limits, fear, relationships, or irreversible costs make decisions emotionally legible. |
| 12 | Battle emotion without system | Characters win because emotion suddenly overrides established limits. | Emotion changes decisions, risk tolerance, or sacrifice, but mechanics and consequences remain valid. |
| 13 | Knowledge discontinuity | A character knows something in one chapter, forgets it when convenient, then learns it again for exposition. | Knowledge state changes only through credible loss, deception, memory failure, or new interpretation. |
| 14 | Timeline discontinuity | Travel, healing, communication, investigation, or institutional response happens faster than the world permits. | The time ledger supports every transition or the prose acknowledges exceptional acceleration and its cause. |
| 15 | Consequence amnesia | A major battle, scandal, death, legal breach, or infrastructure failure disappears from ordinary life immediately after the chapter ends. | Relevant aftermath persists in state even when most of it stays off-page. |
| 16 | Theme speech | Characters or narrator summarize the novel's theme as a clean lesson. | Different people and institutions embody conflicting answers through choices and consequences. |
| 17 | Over-modeling | Architecture keeps expanding into remote trivia while Chapter 1 remains unwritten. Variant (legacy Test 123): The agent keeps elaborating names, remote lore, distant factions, and cosmetic details because "more planning" feels safer, delaying prose indefinitely. | The current causal cone passes the readiness gate and remote areas remain controlled open space. Iteration stops when no blocker remains, remaining unknowns are intentional discovery space, and another pass would mostly add distant trivia rather than protect near-term causality. |
| 18 | Under-modeling | A scene requires law, logistics, cost, power, or institutional behavior that the world model never considered, forcing an instant convenience. | Relevant systems were modeled enough to produce the needed consequence honestly. |
| 19 | Single-clue miracle | A huge reveal depends entirely on one suspiciously perfect clue. | Multiple independent facts or pressures support the truth, even if only one becomes decisive in the moment. |
| 20 | Scene without delta | A full scene ends with the same plan, knowledge, relationship, leverage, body, resource, status, emotional baseline, and reader interpretation with which it began. | The scene creates at least one meaningful state or interpretive change, or deepens a durable lived baseline enough to justify page time; otherwise it is cut, merged, or summarized. |
| 21 | Over-bridged narration | Every transition is announced with “then,” “just then,” “suddenly,” “the next moment,” “he noticed,” “the system appeared,” or an equivalent sentence even when the following line already makes the transition obvious. | Causal steps still exist, but obvious transitions are safely inferred. Bridge language remains only where temporal, spatial, logical, or viewpoint orientation genuinely requires it. |
| 22 | Equal-weight event narration | A joke, a routine arrival, a legal threat, a major reveal, and a life-changing choice all receive the same setup-reaction-explanation structure and similar paragraph weight. | Narrative space and reaction style reflect meaningful change. Minor events can pass quickly; major changes alter attention, timing, interpretation, or behavior without necessarily becoming verbose. |
| 23 | Metronomic web-fiction rhythm | The prose mechanically alternates short and long sentences, inserts a hook every fixed interval, uses one-sentence paragraphs as default, or manufactures reversals to maintain a visible beat pattern. | Rhythm follows expectation, viewpoint, pressure, and consequence. Repetition and silence arise from the scene rather than a timer. |
| 24 | Explanation echo | Action or image conveys the meaning, then narration, internal thought, dialogue, or system text restates the same meaning in adjacent form. | The prose trusts inferable meaning. Explanation remains only when it adds a new layer, corrects a plausible misreading, or reflects viewpoint thinking that itself matters. |
| 25 | Intelligence theatre negotiation | A political meeting feels sophisticated because everyone speaks in riddles, trades cutting lines, and instantly detects every implication, but nobody controls a meaningful resource, procedure, jurisdiction, record, outside option, or cost. | The conflict remains legible if the clever phrasing is stripped away: participants have different authority, information, dependencies, risks, and fallback options, and their moves alter the available option set. |
| 26 | Telepathic subtext | Every character understands the hidden meaning of every sentence immediately, and the narrator then explains that meaning to the reader as well. | Inference depends on actual shared history, expertise, status, evidence, or context. Some participants miss, misread, or only partly understand implications. The narrator leaves inferable subtext unstated unless the viewpoint genuinely thinks through it. |
| 27 | Procedural decoration | The meeting contains votes, records, titles, precedents, motions, or audits only as atmosphere; the protagonist could ignore them and obtain the same result through a clever speech or display of strength. | Procedure changes jurisdiction, evidence thresholds, timing, responsibility, remedies, public commitment, or future precedent. Ignoring it would materially change or endanger the objective. |
| 28 | Narrative overcoverage | The simulation is rich, so the prose shows every trip, briefing, training session, investigation, meeting, and reaction. Nothing is technically filler, yet the novel feels exhaustive and flat. | The world continues offscreen. Full scenes are reserved for events whose direct experience changes state, interpretation, attachment, pressure, or durable baseline. Routine causality is summarized, implied, or allowed to arrive later through consequence. |
| 29 | Reaction-harvesting POV | The viewpoint repeatedly switches only to show that another character is shocked, impressed, angry, jealous, or afraid of the protagonist. | A POV switch changes available knowledge, uncertainty, decision, stakes, or social meaning. Redundant reactions remain outside the text or appear indirectly. |
| 30 | Feature-checklist scene | A scene visibly exists to satisfy “one daily-life beat, one joke, one clue, one political line, one emotional moment,” making the authorial checklist more visible than the characters' actual needs. | The scene arises from causal action and local objectives. Tone, daily life, humor, politics, setup, and emotion appear only where the situation naturally generates them. |
| 31 | Wrong-layer revision | A chapter feels weak, so the revision adds stronger metaphors, shorter sentences, more hooks, or prettier dialogue even though the real problem is bad scene selection, broken motivation, missing pressure, or insufficient world support. | The revision identifies the highest failing layer first and repairs structure before prose polish. |
| 32 | Unsafe ellipsis | In an attempt to create speed or rhythm, the prose skips the decisive motive, choice, evidence, resource acquisition, relationship transition, spatial fact, or causal bridge required to make a later outcome fair. | Ellipsis removes inferable or low-value bridges. Necessary motive, fairness, spatial clarity, evidence, and causal support remain on the page or are securely established earlier. |
| 33 | Information-only scenery | The prose names scale, weather, architecture, damage, or landscape facts, but the reader cannot actually picture surfaces, light, distance, material, motion, or spatial relation. The setting functions as a data layer rather than a place. | Selected physical details make the environment perceptible without requiring exhaustive description. The reader can form a usable spatial/material impression, and the location remains present while action continues. |
| 34 | Metaphor substitution | Every object is introduced through a simile, personification, or symbolic comparison and then abandoned. The prose contains images but no durable physical world. | Important objects can survive the removal of their metaphors because literal form, material, surface, light, position, or behavior has been established. Figurative language adds perception rather than replacing it. |
| 35 | Plot-owned environment | Every visible object is a clue, omen, symbol, thematic device, or future payoff. The world feels constructed only for the protagonist and the current plot. | Some details remain mundane, irrelevant, or merely habitual. The environment has maintenance, wear, background motion, and ordinary objects that would exist even if the main plot never arrived. |
| 36 | Empty dialogue chamber | A room or landscape receives one establishing sentence, then disappears while characters exchange dialogue and internal monologue. No one moves relative to objects, and light, weather, sound, machinery, or background life stops existing. | The environment persists selectively through action and time without interrupting dialogue for decorative inserts. |
| 37 | Descriptive resolution mismatch | The prose gives equal detail to everything, or rushes past the one object/location that readers actually need to see clearly. Variant (legacy Test 76): Every new room, street, meal, vehicle, landscape, and object receives roughly the same descriptive weight regardless of novelty, spatial need, urgency, emotional duration, or later consequence. | Resolution follows narrative and perceptual importance. Some elements remain broad; selected focal objects receive concrete sustained attention when their material presence matters. Description shifts deliberately between mention, anchor, dwell, and inspection according to the current perceptual and narrative job. |
| 38 | Mechanical scale cutting | Macro and human viewpoints alternate on a visible schedule, or human cutaways exist only to register shock at the large event. | Hard cuts occur when they change scale, knowledge, social meaning, or emotional weight. Their cadence is irregular, and each human viewpoint retains a local objective and material life independent of the macro spectacle. |
| 39 | Descriptive abort | The narration points at an object or landscape, gives one adjective or metaphor, and immediately returns to dialogue/action. The prose repeatedly asks the reader to look without giving enough literal information to complete the perception. | When the narration deliberately zooms in, it stays long enough to establish a small cluster of concrete form, material, spatial relation, use, wear, motion, light, or other relevant physical evidence before moving on. |
| 40 | Generic literary camera | Different viewpoints describe rooms, streets, bodies, machines, and landscapes with the same aesthetic attention and metaphor vocabulary. Description sounds like one omnipresent stylist rather than perception shaped by profession, age, fear, habit, class, or familiarity. | Attention differs by viewpoint. What is noticed, ignored, measured, remembered, or considered abnormal changes even when sentence style remains coherent across the novel. |
| 41 | Decorative human cutaway | A cosmic, political, military, or disaster-scale passage cuts to a random person only so they can stare, panic, praise, or confirm the scale of the spectacle. The person has no local life beyond reaction. | A brief or one-shot human viewpoint has an independent local task, body, relationship, job, route, inconvenience, or obligation. The macro event interrupts or reframes an already existing life rather than creating a disposable reaction face. |
| 42 | Description by quota | Revision notices thin scenery and responds by inserting smell, sound, texture, weather, and background motion into every scene regardless of viewpoint or importance. Prose becomes dutiful, slow, and equally textured. | Descriptive resolution varies. Some scenes need only one anchor; selected places or objects receive sustained attention because scale, action, baseline, emotion, or lived reality makes that attention worthwhile. |
| 43 | Action-smuggled scenery | The prose believes description must always be hidden inside character action, so people constantly touch, inspect, cross, open, or manipulate objects merely to justify describing them. The scene feels busy and still never simply looks. | Action reveals physical properties when action is naturally happening, but the narration also allows direct sustained description when perception, scale, orientation, atmosphere, or material curiosity makes looking itself worthwhile. |

## B. Hidden causality, character change, reader memory, organizations, and contingency

| ID | Failure family | Fail if | Pass if |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| 44 | Static hidden conspiracy | A secret faction, antagonist, or scheme exists only as a fixed answer and does not change while the protagonist interferes with its plans. | The hidden process has an objective, current plan, information state, fallback behavior, and memory of interference. Failure changes its future behavior. |
| 45 | Naked adversarial experiment | An antagonist performs an oddly clean sequence of attacks or plan variants whose only purpose is obviously to test the protagonist's power or knowledge. | Every probe has credible first-order utility as normal strategy. The experimental purpose is a second layer that becomes legible mainly in retrospect. |
| 46 | Prevention equals resolution | The protagonist prevents one predicted disaster and the story treats the underlying threat as gone without removing the actor, objective, capability, or opportunity. | The hidden cause either remains active, adapts, is independently neutralized, or produces clear residue. Quietness is not automatically safety. |
| 47 | Cosmetic counterfactual branch | A reset, alternate branch, or repeated scenario changes victims, locations, or spectacle but teaches nothing new about the causal structure. | The comparison changes a causal hypothesis by revealing an invariant, a dependency, an adaptive response, or a previously hidden mechanism. |
| 48 | Adversary amnesia | An opponent repeatedly experiences impossible timing, leaked plans, or preempted actions but never updates their model of the protagonist. | The opponent forms plausible hypotheses, tests or narrows them when useful, changes security or tactics, and remembers what prior interference taught them. |
| 49 | Mastermind-stack depth | Every reveal becomes "there was an even higher secret mastermind" instead of deepening mechanism, motive, history, institution, or system. | Major reveals often move vertically from event to mechanism to strategy to systemic cause. Additional hidden actors appear only when independently justified. |
| 50 | Consequence-free information power | Prediction, prophecy, mind reading, surveillance, or similar abilities function as free camera access and simply remove uncertainty. | The ability has defined scope, cost, failure modes, detectability or side channels where appropriate, counterplay, and downstream social or institutional consequences. |
| 51 | Clean exoneration collapse | A falsely accused character is cleared and every suspicious behavior turns out to be an innocent coincidence, flattening the character and invalidating prior tension. | The central accusation can be wrong while some suspicious facts remain real and belong to different secrets, obligations, costs, affiliations, or mistakes. |
| 52 | Total convergence world | Every side character, local dispute, relationship, and ordinary detail eventually connects to the protagonist or final conspiracy. | Some threads cross the main plot, some brush it, and some remain independent while still changing people, institutions, places, or daily life honestly. |
| 53 | Trait-switch character arc | A character becomes brave, cruel, trusting, selfless, cynical, or mature at the moment the outline needs it, with no intermediate choices, relapses, changed interpretation, or relationship history. | Change appears through pressure, appraisal, behavioral rehearsal, cost, partial relapse, and later choices that carry accumulated history. |
| 54 | One-axis relationship | A relationship is represented only as friend/enemy or trust-high/trust-low, so affection, dependence, fear, debt, shame, and power all move together. | Relevant relationship dimensions can diverge. People may love without trusting, trust without liking, depend while resenting, or become politically opposed while remaining personally intimate. |
| 55 | Reader-known equals reader-active | A payoff depends on a low-salience fact shown hundreds of chapters earlier with no reactivation, and fairness is defended only by saying the clue technically appeared. | The old fact is naturally reactivated before it must be used, or its original salience and recurring consequences make recall genuinely plausible. |
| 56 | Mystery RAM overflow | Too many names, questions, factions, and clues demand simultaneous active recall, making confusion indistinguishable from complexity. | Only a manageable foreground question set stays hot while other threads cool or go dormant with designed reactivation paths. |
| 57 | New-volume amnesia | A new map or volume discards prior injuries, debts, reputation, institutional changes, tactics, relationships, and enemy learning. | The new phase inherits durable state even when the local cast, geography, or objective changes. |
| 58 | Frozen return map | The protagonist returns after years and every shop, institution, friendship, price, generation, and local conflict waits in essentially the same state. | The place shows autonomous change, slow background change, protagonist-caused change, and selective persistence with reasons. |
| 59 | Institutional hive mind | One member discovers something and the whole faction immediately behaves as if it knows, believes, and understands the same thing. | Acquisition, reporting, retention, retrieval, interpretation, and policy change are distinct steps subject to hierarchy, faction, secrecy, incentives, records, and delay. |
| 60 | Infinite tactical patching | An organization suffers repeated anomalies but only keeps adding guards, rules, or tactical patches without ever questioning the assumption causing the failures. | Enough credible anomaly can produce competing models or double-loop learning, while institutional incentives may still delay or distort that shift. |
| 61 | Authorial theme verdict | The story stages a difficult moral or philosophical conflict, then the narrator or obviously privileged character explains the approved answer after the scene. | Competing positions are embodied in choices, institutions, costs, and counterexamples. Consequences can discriminate between claims without reducing every dissenting voice to a straw man. |
| 62 | Total teleology | Every accident, relationship, ordinary object, local dispute, and inconvenience later proves necessary to the central conspiracy or climax. | The world preserves bounded noise, unrelated life, local resolutions, and mixed events whose significance comes from consequences rather than hidden design. |
| 63 | Convenient chance resolution | An accident or coincidence removes the decisive obstacle and resolves a conflict the characters failed to solve. | Chance may trigger, complicate, or alter the state, but consequential resolution still grows from character and institutional response. |

## C. Focalization, dialogue, complexity, and adversarial robustness

| ID | Failure family | Fail if | Pass if |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| 64 | Camera-permission leak | A restricted scene states another character's hidden motive, an offscreen fact, or a future consequence as certainty even though neither the focal character nor the established narrator can know it. | Information stays inside the established focalization regime, or the narrator's broader access has already been established and is used deliberately. |
| 65 | Artificial thought withholding | A viewpoint character already knows the decisive secret but narration carefully avoids the obvious thought until the reveal solely to manipulate the reader. | Withholding follows believable denial, taboo, distraction, memory failure, strategic self-censorship, or another established psychological reason; otherwise the thought is allowed to exist. |
| 66 | Mechanical POV carousel | The novel rotates A/B/C viewpoints because each character is "due," even when the next switch adds no causal, emotional, strategic, or thematic value. | POV changes are selected by narrative gain, and lines may remain dormant for long stretches without violating world continuity. |
| 67 | Duplicate-event retelling | The same event is replayed from another viewpoint with mostly identical information and emotional meaning. | The second view changes causal understanding, moral interpretation, hidden cost, social meaning, or trust in the first account. |
| 68 | Repeated-tactic scene | Characters keep arguing, fighting, interrogating, or searching with the same method while intensity merely increases. | Resistance causes tactic change, new evidence, altered leverage, changed interpretation, or compression. |
| 69 | Dialogue as information transcript | Characters alternate complete explanatory statements whose primary function is telling the reader facts, despite shared knowledge or lack of social motive. | Dialogue performs requests, refusals, tests, concealment, face management, bargaining, alignment, threat, repair, or other in-world action; exposition enters through genuine asymmetry or consequence. |
| 70 | Universal polished voice | Every intelligent character speaks in equally compressed, witty, metaphorically elegant, quotable lines. | Intelligence appears through different reasoning styles, evidence preferences, directness, rhythm, register, uncertainty tolerance, and social strategies. |
| 71 | Audience-invariant speech | A character speaks with the same directness, vocabulary, emotional openness, and turn-taking behavior to a lover, superior, subordinate, stranger, and public hearing. | Stable identity coexists with audience-dependent register, face work, risk management, and intimacy. |
| 72 | Node inflation | Whenever the story needs more depth it adds another faction, hidden master, named side character, artifact, prophecy, POV, or rule. | The design first tests whether deeper relationships, internal factions, information asymmetry, resource dependence, history, or changed alliances among existing elements can create the needed complexity. |
| 73 | Over-pruned living world | Revision removes every detail, side life, object, joke, routine, and local event that does not pay off in the main plot, leaving a perfectly efficient but artificial world. | Low-cost ordinary life, bounded noise, social texture, and independent side-character existence remain where they earn experiential value. |
| 74 | Protected climax | A climax works only because opponents ignore a cheaper move, institutions fail to react, an ally cooperates without incentive, logistics become frictionless, or the public does not notice. | A red-team review finds no obvious unaddressed exploit, or the story establishes credible constraints, information limits, costs, values, or institutional reasons that close it. |

## D. Environment, spatial attention, and dynamic vividness

| ID | Failure family | Fail if | Pass if |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| 77 | Mechanical scanpath | Scenery is repeatedly described left-to-right, top-to-bottom, or near-to-far simply because the prose needs an orderly list. | Descriptive direction follows viewpoint task, movement, figure-ground relation, depth, scale, reveal, or meaningful spatial hierarchy; some scenes remain distributed rather than directional. |
| 78 | Detail-before-field disorientation | An unfamiliar environment begins with several disconnected close-ups, leaving readers unable to place bodies, exits, distance, or scale even though disorientation is not intended. | The prose supplies enough scene gist or later widening for local details to belong to a usable spatial model; deliberate disorientation is clearly motivated by viewpoint or effect. |
| 79 | Scenic inventory | A descriptive paragraph names everything visible with equal weight, producing a catalogue rather than attention. | A few semantically or spatially diagnostic anchors imply the larger field, while closer detail is reserved for what the viewpoint or scene has reason to process. |
| 80 | Urgency-insensitive description | A fleeing, fighting, injured, or time-pressured viewpoint pauses for leisurely architectural description that neither reflects altered time perception nor serves immediate action. | Descriptive bandwidth contracts, fragments, or becomes task-driven under urgency unless the narrative deliberately slows subjective time for an established reason. |
| 81 | Re-entry reset | Every return to a familiar place repeats the original establishing description as if the reader and viewpoint have never seen it. | Routine returns use names or anchors; meaningful returns emphasize changed, missing, repaired, aged, seasonally altered, or newly salient features. |
| 82 | Unprepared transformation | A place is destroyed, abandoned, occupied, renovated, or corrupted and the text expects emotional or tactical impact even though the reader never received enough earlier baseline to understand what changed. | When contrast matters, prior scenes establish a selective normal state or stable landmarks against which transformation can be perceived. |
| 83 | Disembodied description drone | Restricted POV suddenly glides across ceilings, rooftops, hidden corners, distant faces, and tiny objects in a camera-like sweep unrelated to what the focal character could perceive or attend to. | Spatial coverage respects focalization and attention, or a broader narratorial camera has already been established and is used deliberately. |
| 84 | Action-description block alternation | A kinetic scene repeatedly stops for static scenery, then resumes generic action, making description and event feel like separate layers. | When motion matters, selected environmental details enter because bodies, vehicles, objects, or attention interact with them; direct scenic pauses are reserved for moments that genuinely need them. |
| 85 | Kinetic teleportation | Bodies are suddenly on the ground, across the room, facing another direction, or behind cover without a legible change in balance, route, obstruction, or trajectory. | Important physical transitions preserve enough micro-causal geometry for the reader to understand how the new position resulted from the previous one. |
| 86 | Shock omniscience | A frightened, injured, falling, or surprised limited viewpoint accurately registers every sound, face, collision, trajectory, and simultaneous event. | Perception narrows, drops channels, misorders impressions, or lags behind interpretation in ways consistent with focalization and bodily state; missing information can be reconstructed later. |
| 87 | Frozen-world impact | Once the protagonist is hit, attacked, or endangered, traffic, crowds, machinery, weather, unrelated people, and institutional processes disappear until the protagonist is ready for them again. Variant (legacy Test 102): Once danger starts, slow environmental or infrastructural processes disappear completely until the action ends. | A small number of independent background processes continue or react, proving that the event is embedded in an autonomous environment. Selected slow processes continue underneath the foreground, providing continuity without stealing attention. |
| 88 | Pre-highlighted accident machinery | Every object that will matter in an accident is conspicuously introduced beforehand, making the causal chain feel authored, symbolic, or mechanically foreshadowed. | Ordinary baseline details receive ordinary attention; incidental obstacles or triggers may become important only when contact, motion, or changed attention makes them relevant. |
| 89 | Impact without new state | A crash, fall, explosion, or strike functions as a punctuation mark: characters immediately talk or continue with no changed orientation, pain, debris, route, crowd behavior, or environmental consequence. | The event leaves a legible new physical and social state before the narrative decides how much of the aftermath to compress. |
| 90 | Universal slow motion | Every punch, stumble, near miss, dropped object, and collision receives frame-by-frame kinetic description. | The prose chooses between glance, tracking, compact causal chain, and aftermath dwell according to consequence, legibility, emotional weight, and pacing. |
| 91 | Verb pile without situation update | A chase, fight, accident, or escape contains many action verbs, but the reader cannot say what changed in location, orientation, route, object control, sensory access, or tactical possibility. | Major kinetic beats update a legible situation model; low-consequence motion is compressed. |
| 92 | Protagonist-gravity environment | Nearby people, vehicles, machinery, and routines exist only to notice, assist, obstruct, or react to the protagonist. | At least some nearby processes have independent reasons to continue, ignore, lag behind, or intersect with the protagonist only when their paths genuinely meet. |
| 93 | Theme-park background motion | The author inserts constant quirky background activity to prove the world is alive, creating visual noise with no persistence or causal/social relation. | A small number of background processes persist long enough to feel autonomous and matter through continuity, contrast, obstruction, or delayed contact. |
| 94 | Affordance-free object | Objects in action scenes are described as shape, color, and material but never in relation to what bodies can do with, around, through, or against them. | When relevant, object description includes contextual action possibilities or constraints without turning every object into a tactical prop. |
| 95 | Impossible visibility | The focal viewpoint tracks targets continuously through crowds, walls, vehicles, smoke, falls, or head/body turns that should break line of sight. | Occlusion, posture, orientation, and changing sensory access create real information loss and sometimes force repositioning or inference. |
| 96 | Synchronized crowd response | A public disturbance causes everyone to gasp, turn, run, understand, or help at once. | Responses vary by proximity, role, knowledge, risk, mobility, responsibility, and delay; only a few representative reactions are shown. |
| 97 | Zero-latency consequence | Impact, pain, interpretation, bystander response, emergency action, rumor, and institutional reaction all appear immediately. | Consequences propagate at different plausible speeds; body, mind, local social field, and institution need not update simultaneously. |
| 98 | Cross-thread collision without prior life | A vehicle, passerby, animal, machine, or secondary actor appears only at the instant needed to collide with the protagonist's action. | When a collision between processes matters, each process has an independent trajectory, task, or routine before contact, even if only minimally visible. |
| 99 | Stress-effect template | Every dangerous event automatically produces tunnel vision, ringing ears, slow motion, blurred edges, and delayed pain. | Perceptual narrowing or omission is selective, character- and task-dependent, and never used to hide cues the viewpoint would obviously perceive. |
| 100 | Physics lecture action | The prose pauses to explain vectors, force, momentum, anatomy, or mechanics beyond what the reader needs to understand the event. | Physical causality is carried by observable contact, obstruction, support, trajectory, balance, and consequence; technical explanation appears only when viewpoint or plot justifies it. |
| 101 | Public event with one meaning | A shared urban interruption is immediately given one authoritative interpretation by everyone present. | Different observers may receive the same event through different private concerns, assumptions, social positions, or incomplete sensory access. |
| 103 | Aftermath scale mismatch | A minor stumble triggers pages of social/institutional fallout, or a major public disaster leaves only one sentence of physical consequence before the plot resets. | Aftermath expands through bodily, local-social, institutional, and long-memory rings only to the scale justified by the event. |

## E. Inertia, time, rumor, closure, restraint, and structural repetition

| ID | Failure family | Fail if | Pass if |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| 104 | Plot-synchronized state reset | A relationship, wound, fear, scandal, policy, habit, or public belief changes exactly when the next plot beat needs it, despite forces that should sustain the previous state. | Consequential change has identifiable pressure, constraint, update speed, residue, and where relevant lag, rebound, or path dependence. |
| 105 | Synchronous-layer update | New evidence instantly changes belief, emotion, habit, institutional record, public reputation, and behavior at the same speed. | Different layers update at plausible and story-relevant rates without artificial delay. |
| 106 | Calendar-time pacing | Long in-world periods automatically receive long narration and short events automatically receive short narration. | Scene, summary, ellipsis, and dilation are allocated by change, uncertainty, embodiment, and reader need rather than clock duration. |
| 107 | Recurrence replay | Training sessions, recurring anomalies, work shifts, meetings, or repeated arguments are dramatized at similar resolution every time. | Repetition is compressed once the pattern is known and returns to scene-level detail when variation, adaptation, or breakage matters. |
| 108 | Rumor copy machine | A story passes through many people or media while retaining the same wording, emphasis, certainty, and interpretation. | Important transmission chains reflect selective loss, emphasis, assimilation, medium, motive, and source dependence. |
| 109 | Source-count illusion | Many characters repeat one claim and the narrative treats repetition as independent corroboration. | Source genealogy distinguishes independent observation from descendants of one contaminated account. |
| 110 | Instant reputation correction | A public accusation is disproven and social behavior, records, jokes, hiring, fear, or status immediately return to the previous baseline. | Correction can change belief quickly while reputational or institutional residue persists only as long as its actual supports justify. |
| 111 | Binary closure | An ending is judged only as fully open or fully closed, so every axis is either wrapped up or left unresolved together. | Event, causal, epistemic, relational, emotional, institutional, ethical, identity, threat, and world closure may resolve in different directions. |

## P. Long-term operability and semantic integrity

### P1. Manifest identity split
**Failure:** Project Manifest and Runtime Manifest claim different project IDs or live branches, yet drafting continues.
**Catch:** `project_ops.py verify` must fail before resume/drafting.

### P2. Resume-capsule canon drift
**Failure:** A hand-maintained recap becomes a competing source of truth.
**Catch:** regenerate Resume Capsule from canonical artifacts; never write state back from the capsule.

### P3. Semantic paraphrase leak
**Failure:** a Writer summary avoids forbidden words but paraphrases a backend secret.
**Catch:** propagate `tainted_by` through `derived_from` and block unauthorized roles until explicit declassification.

### P4. Unsupported prose-based delta
**Failure:** state records a durable change that current prose never establishes.
**Catch:** strict prose-state reconciliation requires evidence-linked committed candidates.

### P5. Forgotten durable prose fact
**Failure:** prose establishes a later-relevant transfer/knowledge/injury but the state commit ignores it.
**Catch:** prose candidate must be committed, explicitly non-durable, rejected, or pending; strict commit forbids pending.

### P6. Stale reconciliation after edit
**Failure:** prose changes after approval but old evidence is reused.
**Catch:** whole-prose SHA mismatch invalidates the old reconciliation.

### P7. Governance overreach
**Failure:** a low-mechanism relationship novel is forced through Runtime/branch/invariant machinery.
**Catch:** Lean profile verifies without Runtime and no genre name auto-escalates governance.

### P8. Corpus monoculture
**Failure:** improvements on one realistic mystery corpus cause unrelated genres to inherit its hidden-network/institutional/reveal architecture.
