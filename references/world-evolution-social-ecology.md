# World Evolution and Social Ecology

> SELECTABLE POETICS / WORLD CAPABILITY. Activate when autonomous social/place evolution materially belongs to the project. Do not force broad offscreen ecology into deliberately narrow, allegorical, or consciousness-centered work.

Use this reference when a setting must feel older than the protagonist, continue changing offscreen, and support informal social life beyond formal institutions.

## Core principle

The world does not merely process plot events; it accumulates history.

Places, organizations, neighborhoods, technologies, habits, prices, reputations, and generations should change at different speeds. Many changes should occur without the protagonist causing them.

## 1. World-aging domains

For places that recur over long spans, track only domains that can later matter:

- population and migration;
- family/generation structure;
- economy, prices, employment, trade;
- institutions and leadership;
- law and enforcement practice;
- infrastructure and maintenance;
- technology and techniques;
- ecology, climate, disease, resource depletion;
- architecture and land use;
- slang, etiquette, fashion, ritual;
- public memory and official history;
- local status hierarchy;
- transport and communication;
- ordinary services and shops.

Different domains have different inertia.

## 2. Path dependence

Current conditions should reflect prior choices and sunk structures.

A war can leave:

- roads built for armies;
- widows and missing workers;
- debt;
- veteran networks;
- abandoned forts;
- emergency laws that were never fully repealed;
- altered trade routes;
- new elites;
- commemorations and competing memories.

Later shocks should interact with this residue rather than striking a blank slate.

## 3. Lag and asynchronous change

Do not update the whole world at once.

Examples:

- law changes quickly, custom slowly;
- technology spreads unevenly by wealth and geography;
- prices react before institutions do;
- official narratives change before private memory;
- buildings outlive the organizations that built them;
- a generation remembers a war that younger people treat as ancient politics.

Asynchrony creates texture and conflict.

## 4. Place memory

A recurring place should store biography.

Track selected layers:

- what used to be here;
- what was demolished, repaired, renamed, or repurposed;
- who remembers the old version;
- what public story is told about the change;
- what private story survives;
- which routines disappeared;
- which new routines formed;
- what physical residue remains visible.

Return scenes become stronger when the protagonist's memory is only one layer among many.

## 5. Generational turnover

Over multi-year stories, let children age, apprentices gain authority, veterans retire, fashions shift, and historical events change meaning.

A ten-year time span should produce more than stronger combat statistics.

Ask:

- Who entered adulthood?
- Who lost influence?
- Which careers matured?
- Which old disputes became inherited stories?
- Which once-radical idea became normal?
- What does the new generation misunderstand about events the protagonist remembers directly?

## 6. Social ecology beyond institutions

Map informal ties that change access and behavior:

- kinship;
- friendship;
- romance and ex-romance;
- neighbors;
- classmates;
- colleagues;
- patrons and clients;
- mentors and former mentors;
- shared hometown;
- religious community;
- hobby or trade circles;
- old military units;
- debt and favor networks;
- gossip channels;
- brokers who connect otherwise separate groups.

Formal prohibition does not imply practical impossibility if informal access exists.

## 7. Tie properties

For relevant social links, know:

- strength;
- trust;
- frequency;
- social cost of asking a favor;
- information capacity;
- willingness to transmit sensitive information;
- whether the tie bridges different groups;
- whether using it becomes visible to others.

Do not assume information spreads instantly just because a network path exists.

## 8. Rumor ecology

When news travels, track transformations:

- source;
- first audience;
- motive to share or suppress;
- distortion through retelling;
- credibility markers;
- group boundaries;
- official confirmation or denial;
- who benefits from a particular version.

Rumor is not only degraded information. It can become a social fact that changes behavior even when false. For important source chains, public versions, and reputation feedback, use `social-transmission-reputation.md`.

## 9. Autonomous ordinary change

Include changes that are not setup for the main plot:

- a bakery closes;
- a minor official retires;
- a neighborhood becomes expensive;
- a local festival changes sponsor;
- a friend moves away;
- a bus route changes;
- a family has a child;
- a once-fashionable place becomes embarrassing.

These details should occasionally matter to characters, but they need not reveal a conspiracy.

## 10. Return test

When the protagonist returns after meaningful absence, ask:

**What changed that would have changed even if the protagonist never existed?**

If the answer is nothing, the setting has been frozen. If an old state should still resist change after new facts arrive, use `state-inertia.md` rather than forcing instant update.

Then ask:

- What changed because of the protagonist?
- What changed because of independent actors?
- What changed because of slow background processes?
- What failed to change despite pressure, and why?

## 11. Audit

- Do places accumulate visible history?
- Are institutional and cultural changes occurring at plausible speeds?
- Does informal access complicate formal rules?
- Are old relationships still the only relationships side characters possess?
- Does information diffuse according to trust and incentives rather than author convenience?
- Do some local changes remain permanently unrelated to the main plot?
